\medskip\noindent\textbf{What is and is not formalized.}
The chapter has three parts, all sorry-free.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \emph{deterministic layer} (Sections~\ref{sec:dd:risk}--\ref{sec:dd:gram}): exact finite-sample bias--variance identities for linear estimators, the three pseudoinverse representations of ridgeless
  random-feature regression, the trace and bias reductions that bring the variance and bias to a Gaussian matrix $G=XQ$ with $Q^\top Q=I$, the limits of the resulting \emph{explicit} ratio expressions along $n_0/m\to\gamma$, $n/m\to\delta$, and the global shape
  of the limiting risk curve (divergence at $\delta=1$, second descent, flat segment for $\gamma<1$).
  \item The \emph{probabilistic layer} (Sections~\ref{sec:dd:moments} and~\ref{sec:dd:gaussian}): the $\chi^2$ tail bound, orthogonal invariance of Gaussian matrices, almost-sure full rank, the exact law $1/\chi^2_{p-q}$ of the diagonal of $(G^\top G)^{-1}$ and
  its mean, non-asymptotic and in-probability concentration of $\operatorname{Tr}(G^\top G)^{-1}$, the exact $\chi^2$ law of the Rayleigh quotient of $(G^\top G)^{-1}$ and the resulting fit-error concentration, the omitted signal of a Gaussian subspace, and
  the convergence \emph{in probability} of the variance and of the bias of the feature-bottleneck estimator to $\sigma^2\delta/(1-\delta)$ and $\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho_\star$, and of the variance and of the bias of the sample-bottleneck estimator to $\sigma^2\bigl((\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}\bigr)$ and $(1-\gamma^{-1})\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}\rho_\star$.
  \item The \emph{global theorem} (Section~\ref{sec:dd:global}): the minimum-norm operator and the conditional risk as Lean definitions, convergence in probability of the risk in each of the three regimes, and Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:globalDoubleDescent}, that the risk converges in probability to the limiting curve $\mathsf R(\gamma,\delta)$ on the whole non-singular domain.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Not formalized:} the behaviour at the singular set $\{\gamma,\delta\ge1,\ \gamma=1\vee\delta=1\}$ (where the limiting risk is infinite), convergence of the risk in expectation over $(X,S)$ (all limits are in probability; the expectation over the noise is built into the conditional risk), and non-Gaussian designs; see Remark~\ref{rmk:dd:remaining}. The ``limit'' theorems of
Sections~\ref{sec:dd:feature}--\ref{sec:dd:sample} are limits of explicit finite-sample expressions such as $\sigma^2n/(m-n-1)$; the identification of these expressions with the \emph{random} variance is the content of Section~\ref{sec:dd:gaussian}, and the combination into the risk the content of Section~\ref{sec:dd:global}.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Notation.}
The data are $m$ samples with inputs $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ (ambient dimension $n_0$) and labels
$y=X\theta_\star+\varepsilon$, where the noise satisfies $\mathbb E\varepsilon=0$, $\operatorname{Cov}\varepsilon=\sigma^2I_m$.
The random features are $Z=XS\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ with a projection $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ ($n$ features), and
\[
  \gamma:=\frac{n_0}{m},\qquad \delta:=\frac nm,\qquad \rho_\star:=\|\theta_\star\|^2 .
\]
All matrices are real. Index types are arbitrary finite types in the algebraic lemmas, and the Lean statements use
Mathlib's matrix inverse $A^{-1}$, which is the junk value $0$ for singular $A$; every statement that needs $A^{-1}$ to
be a true inverse assumes \texttt{IsUnit A.det} explicitly.


\section{The linear model: risk, bias and variance}\label{sec:dd:risk}

\paragraph{Notation: one parameter, one estimator.}
Throughout the chapter the unknown parameter is $\theta_\star\in\mathbb R^p$ and an estimator of it is $\hat\theta$. The Lean file \texttt{HMRT.lean}, which follows Hastie et al.\ (2022), calls the same objects
$\beta$ and $\hat\beta$ (\texttt{β}, \texttt{β\_hat}), and the Lean files of this chapter write \texttt{θ}, \texttt{θ\_star}, \texttt{β\_hat}. They are the same thing: \emph{$\hat\beta$ in a Lean statement of \texttt{HMRT.lean} and $\hat\theta$ in this chapter are the
same estimator, and $\beta$ and $\theta_\star$ the same parameter}. Dimensions follow the same rule.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.26\textwidth}p{0.17\textwidth}p{0.20\textwidth}}
object & this chapter & \texttt{HMRT.lean} & Lean in \texttt{Basic.lean}\\ \hline
parameter & $\theta_\star$ & $\beta$ & \texttt{θ}, \texttt{θ\_star}\\
estimator & $\hat\theta$ & $\hat\beta$ & \texttt{β\_hat}\\
parameter dimension & $p$ (in the random-feature application $p=n_0$) & $p$ & \texttt{p}\\
number of samples & $m$ & $n$ & \texttt{n}\\
feature covariance, noise covariance & $\Sigma$, $\Gamma$ & \texttt{Sigma} & \texttt{Sigma}, \texttt{Γ}
\end{tabular}

So the generic statements of this section are written with parameter dimension $p$ and $m$ samples; the sample index type of the Lean lemmas is called \texttt{n} there, while from Section~\ref{sec:dd:features} on the Lean files use \texttt{m} for samples, \texttt{n₀} for the ambient dimension and \texttt{n} for the number of features.

\begin{definition}[Linear model and linear estimator]\label{def:dd:linearModel}
In the \emph{linear model} the labels are $y=X\theta_\star+\varepsilon\in\mathbb R^m$, where $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times p}$ is the design matrix, $\theta_\star\in\mathbb R^p$ is a fixed parameter and the noise $\varepsilon\in\mathbb R^m$ is a random vector with
$\mathbb E\varepsilon=0$ and covariance $\operatorname{Cov}\varepsilon=\Gamma$ (isotropic noise: $\Gamma=\sigma^2I_m$). The design $X$ is treated as fixed, so all randomness is in $\varepsilon$.
An estimator $\hat\theta$ of $\theta_\star$ is \emph{linear} if there is a matrix $M\in\mathbb R^{p\times m}$, which may depend on $X$ (and on a projection $S$ in the random-feature setting) but not on $\varepsilon$, with
\[
  \hat\theta=My=M(X\theta_\star+\varepsilon).
\]
Subtracting $\theta_\star$ gives the \emph{error partition}
\[
  \hat\theta-\theta_\star=\underbrace{(MX-I)\theta_\star}_{\text{fixed}}+\underbrace{M\varepsilon}_{\text{centered}},
\]
a deterministic vector plus a centered random vector; it is just the distributive law $M(X\theta_\star+\varepsilon)=MX\theta_\star+M\varepsilon$ and has no separate Lean statement.
\end{definition}

This is not a Lean definition: there is no declaration for ``linear model'' or ``linear estimator''. Every Lean theorem takes an arbitrary estimator \texttt{β\_hat : (n → ℝ) → EuclideanSpace ℝ p} (a function of the noise) together with the hypothesis
\texttt{hβ : ∀ ε, (β\_hat ε).ofLp = M *ᵥ (X *ᵥ θ.ofLp + ε)}, and the noise assumptions as the hypotheses \texttt{h\_mean} (centered) and \texttt{h\_cov} (covariance $\Gamma$). Definition~\ref{def:dd:linearModel} is exactly the conjunction of these hypotheses,
and a theorem below that says ``let $\hat\theta=M(X\theta_\star+\varepsilon)$'' assumes it.

\begin{definition}[Risk, bias, covariance and variance of an estimator]\label{def:dd:risk}
  \lean{LinearRegression.risk, LinearRegression.bias, LinearRegression.covCondX, LinearRegression.variance}
  \leanok
Let $\hat\theta$ be a random vector in $\mathbb R^p$ (the estimator, random through the noise) and $\Sigma\in\mathbb R^{p\times p}$ a
weighting matrix (the feature covariance). Its \emph{risk}, \emph{bias} and \emph{variance} are
\[
  R(\hat\theta;\theta_\star)=\mathbb E\,(\hat\theta-\theta_\star)^\top\Sigma(\hat\theta-\theta_\star),\qquad
  B(\hat\theta;\theta_\star)=(\mathbb E\hat\theta-\theta_\star)^\top\Sigma(\mathbb E\hat\theta-\theta_\star),\qquad
  V(\hat\theta)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta)\,\Sigma\bigr),
\]
where $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta)_{jk}=\operatorname{cov}(\hat\theta_j,\hat\theta_k)$. In Lean these are \texttt{LinearRegression.risk}, \texttt{bias}, \texttt{covCondX} and \texttt{variance}, with $\theta_\star$ and $\hat\theta$ named \texttt{β} and \texttt{β\_hat}.
They make sense for any estimator and say nothing about linearity.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Second moments of a square-integrable random vector]\label{lem:dd:secondMoments}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.integral_dotProduct_mulVec_self,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.integral_dotProduct_mul_dotProduct}
  \leanok
Let $x$ be a random vector in $\mathbb R^p$ with square-integrable coordinates and second-moment matrix
$\Gamma_{jk}=\mathbb E[x_jx_k]$. For every matrix $S$ and vectors $a,b$,
\[
  \mathbb E\,[x^\top Sx]=\operatorname{Tr}(S\Gamma),\qquad \mathbb E\,[(x\cdot a)(x\cdot b)]=a^\top\Gamma b .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Write $x^\top Sx=\sum_{j,k}S_{jk}x_jx_k$; the products $x_jx_k$ are integrable by Cauchy--Schwarz, so linearity of the
integral gives $\sum_{jk}S_{jk}\Gamma_{jk}=\operatorname{Tr}(S\Gamma)$ (using $\Gamma=\Gamma^\top$). For the bilinear form apply the
first identity to $S=ba^\top$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Excess prediction risk is a $\Sigma$-norm]\label{lem:dd:excessRisk}
  \uses{lem:dd:secondMoments}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.excess_prediction_risk_eq_mahalanobis,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.excess_prediction_risk_eq_param_risk_isotropic}
  \leanok
If a new feature vector $x\sim P$ has square-integrable coordinates and second-moment matrix $\Sigma$, then for every
parameter error $v$,
\[
  \mathbb E_{x\sim P}\,(x\cdot v)^2=v^\top\Sigma v .
\]
In the isotropic case $\Sigma=I$ this is $\|v\|^2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:secondMoments} with $a=b=v$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Risk is bias plus variance]\label{thm:dd:riskBiasVariance}
  \uses{def:dd:risk, lem:dd:secondMoments}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.integral_sub_mul_sub,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.risk_eq_bias_add_variance}
  \leanok
For any estimator $\hat\theta$ with square-integrable coordinates on a probability space,
$R(\hat\theta;\theta_\star)=B(\hat\theta;\theta_\star)+V(\hat\theta)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
For square-integrable $f,g$ one has $\mathbb E[(f-a)(g-b)]=\operatorname{cov}(f,g)+(\mathbb Ef-a)(\mathbb Eg-b)$. Hence the
second-moment matrix of $\hat\theta-\theta_\star$ is $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta)+dd^\top$ with $d=\mathbb E\hat\theta-\theta_\star$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:secondMoments}, $R=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta)+dd^\top)\Sigma\bigr)
=V+d^\top\Sigma d=V+B$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact bias--variance decomposition of a linear estimator]\label{thm:dd:linearBV}
  \uses{def:dd:linearModel, def:dd:risk, thm:dd:riskBiasVariance, lem:dd:secondMoments}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.linearEstimator_bias_variance,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.linearEstimator_bias_variance_isotropic,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.exact_conditional_bias_variance_decomposition}
  \leanok
Let $\hat\theta=M(X\theta_\star+\varepsilon)$ be a linear estimator in the linear model of Definition~\ref{def:dd:linearModel}, with $\varepsilon$ centered, square-integrable, with covariance matrix $\Gamma$.
Then
\[
  B=\bigl((MX-I)\theta_\star\bigr)^\top\Sigma\bigl((MX-I)\theta_\star\bigr),\qquad
  V=\operatorname{Tr}(M\Gamma M^\top\Sigma),\qquad R=B+V .
\]
For isotropic noise $\Gamma=\sigma^2I$ the variance is $\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}(MM^\top\Sigma)$, and for the parameter risk
($\Sigma=I$)
\[
  \mathbb E\|\hat\theta-\theta_\star\|^2=\|(MX-I)\theta_\star\|^2+\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
By the error partition of Definition~\ref{def:dd:linearModel} the estimator is affine in $\varepsilon$: $\mathbb E\hat\theta=MX\theta_\star$ (since $\mathbb E\varepsilon=0$) and
$\hat\theta-\mathbb E\hat\theta=M\varepsilon$, so the bias vector is $\mathbb E\hat\theta-\theta_\star=(MX-I)\theta_\star$. The covariance of $M\varepsilon$ has entries
$M_j\cdot\Gamma M_k=(M\Gamma M^\top)_{jk}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:secondMoments} (applied to $a=M_j,b=M_k$, the rows of $M$).
This gives $B$ and $V$, and $R=B+V$ is Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:riskBiasVariance}. The isotropic statements specialize
$\Gamma=\sigma^2I$ and use $\operatorname{Tr}(MM^\top)=\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Bias and variance of the ridgeless least-squares estimator]\label{thm:dd:hmrtLemma1}
  \uses{thm:dd:linearBV}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.lemma1_of_linear_model,
        Matrix.mul_mul_transpose_apply}
  \leanok
Let $y=X\theta+\varepsilon$ with $n$ samples (the number of samples is called $n$ here, as in \texttt{HMRT.lean}, and $m$ in the rest of the chapter), $\varepsilon$ centered with $\operatorname{Cov}\varepsilon=\sigma^2I_n$, and
$\hat\Sigma=X^\top X/n$. Let $\hat\Sigma^+$ be a symmetric pseudo-inverse in the sense $\hat\Sigma^+\hat\Sigma\hat\Sigma^+=\hat\Sigma^+$ with
$\hat\Sigma^+\hat\Sigma$ symmetric, and $\hat\theta=\hat\Sigma^+X^\top y/n$. With $\Pi=I-\hat\Sigma^+\hat\Sigma$,
\[
  B=\theta^\top\Pi\,\Sigma\,\Pi\theta,\qquad V=\frac{\sigma^2}n\operatorname{Tr}(\hat\Sigma^+\Sigma).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:linearBV} with $M=\hat\Sigma^+X^\top/n$. Then $MX=\hat\Sigma^+\hat\Sigma$ and
$MM^\top=\hat\Sigma^+\hat\Sigma\hat\Sigma^+/n=\hat\Sigma^+/n$ by the pseudo-inverse identity. The entries of the covariance $M\Gamma M^\top$ are
the bilinear forms $M_j\cdot\Gamma M_k$ (\texttt{Matrix.mul\_mul\_transpose\_apply}, the row-wise form of $\operatorname{Cov}(Mx)=M\operatorname{Cov}(x)M^\top$).
\end{proof}


\section{Random features and the three pseudoinverses}\label{sec:dd:features}

\paragraph{The regression problem.}
The data come from the linear model of Definition~\ref{def:dd:linearModel} with parameter dimension $p=n_0$ (the \emph{ambient} dimension): the labels are $y=X\theta_\star+\varepsilon\in\mathbb R^m$, where $\theta_\star\in\mathbb R^{n_0}$ is the unknown teacher,
the $m$ rows of $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ are the sample inputs $x_1,\dots,x_m$, and the noise $\varepsilon$ is centered with covariance $\sigma^2I_m$. The learner does not fit $n_0$ coefficients directly. It first maps every input to $n$
\emph{random features}, $x\mapsto S^\top x\in\mathbb R^n$, using a fixed matrix $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ (in the probabilistic layer, a Gaussian matrix independent of $X$), and then fits coefficients $\eta\in\mathbb R^n$ in feature space.
Stacking the feature vectors of the samples as rows gives the \emph{feature matrix} $Z=XS\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$. The learned predictor is $x\mapsto(S^\top x)\cdot\hat\eta=x\cdot(S\hat\eta)$, so it is also a linear function of the original input, with the
ambient-space coefficient vector $S\hat\eta\in\mathbb R^{n_0}$. This vector is the estimator of $\theta_\star$ whose risk, bias and variance are studied (Definition~\ref{def:dd:risk}).

\begin{definition}[Ridgeless random-feature regression]\label{def:dd:randomFeatures}
  \uses{def:dd:linearModel, def:dd:risk}
Let $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$, let $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ be any matrix (a map from $n_0$ ambient coordinates to $n$ features; it is not assumed to be a projection or to have orthonormal columns), let $y\in\mathbb R^m$ be the labels, and put $Z=XS$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \emph{feature coefficients} $\hat\eta\in\mathbb R^n$ are the minimum-norm minimizer of $\eta\mapsto\|Z\eta-y\|$: among all least-squares solutions it is the one of smallest Euclidean norm. ``Ridgeless'' means that there is no
  penalty term, as the limit of vanishing ridge penalty.
  \item The \emph{random-feature estimator} is $\hat\theta:=S\hat\eta\in\mathbb R^{n_0}$.
  \item Which pseudoinverse of $Z$ gives $\hat\eta$ depends on the regime ($\gamma=n_0/m$, $\delta=n/m$), and in every regime $\hat\theta=My$ for a matrix $M\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times m}$ built only from $X$ and $S$:
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.13\textwidth}p{0.24\textwidth}p{0.27\textwidth}}
regime & invertible & $\hat\eta$ & $M$ (so that $\hat\theta=My$)\\ \hline
feature bottleneck, $\delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}$ & $Z^\top Z$ & $(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y$ & $S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$\\[0.5ex]
ambient bottleneck, $\gamma<1,\ \gamma\le\delta$ & $X^\top X$, $SS^\top$ &
$S^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ & $SS^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$\\[0.5ex]
sample bottleneck, $\gamma,\delta>1$ & $ZZ^\top$ & $Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ & $SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In the ambient regime $S$ has full row rank, so $SS^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}=I$ and $\hat\theta$ is ordinary least squares in the original coordinates. In the feature regime $Z$ has full column rank and $\hat\eta$ is the unique least-squares solution; in the sample regime $Z$ has full row rank,
$Z\hat\eta=y$ interpolates the data, and $\hat\eta$ is the minimum-norm interpolator (Theorems~\ref{thm:dd:minNorm} and~\ref{thm:dd:leastSquares}).

Because $M$ does not depend on $\varepsilon$, $\hat\theta=M(X\theta_\star+\varepsilon)$ is a linear estimator in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:dd:linearModel}, so Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:linearBV} applies with this $M$. The definition itself is mathematical: the per-regime theorems of Sections~\ref{sec:dd:feature}--\ref{sec:dd:sample} take an estimator \texttt{β\_hat} together with the hypothesis \texttt{hβ : ∀ ε, (β\_hat ε).ofLp = M *ᵥ (X *ᵥ θ.ofLp + ε)} with $M$ written out as in the last column, and the invertibility assumptions as \texttt{IsUnit} hypotheses.
The matrix $M$ \emph{is} a Lean definition in the global theorem, where it is chosen by the dimensions (Definition~\ref{def:dd:minNormOperator}).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Derivation of $M$ across the three regimes]\label{rmk:dd:derivationOfM}
  \uses{def:dd:randomFeatures}
In each regime, the matrix $M\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times m}$ is derived by first solving for the feature coefficients $\hat\eta\in\mathbb R^n$ as the minimum-norm minimizer of the least-squares loss $\eta\mapsto\|Z\eta-y\|^2$ (with $Z=XS$), and then applying the feature map $\hat\theta=S\hat\eta$. Because $\hat\eta$ depends linearly on $y$, writing $\hat\eta=Ay$ immediately yields $M=SA$. The derivation in each of the three regimes proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Feature bottleneck ($\delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}$, so $n\le\min\{m,n_0\}$):}
  Here features are the bottleneck. Assuming full column rank, $Z\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ has rank $n$, so the Gram matrix $Z^\top Z\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ is invertible.
  The least-squares objective $\eta\mapsto\|Z\eta-y\|^2$ is strictly convex with gradient $2Z^\top(Z\eta-y)$. Setting the gradient to zero yields the normal equations
  \[
    Z^\top Z\eta = Z^\top y,
  \]
  whose unique solution is $\hat\eta=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y$. Because the least-squares minimizer is already unique, no tie-breaking by Euclidean norm is needed. Mapping to ambient coordinates gives
  \[
    \hat\theta = S\hat\eta = S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y \implies M = S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top.
  \]

  \item \textbf{Sample bottleneck ($\gamma,\delta>1$, so $m\le\min\{n_0,n\}$):}
  Here samples are the bottleneck. Assuming full row rank, $Z\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ has rank $m$, so the dual Gram matrix $ZZ^\top\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}$ is invertible.
  Because $m<n$, the linear system $Z\eta=y$ is underdetermined and admits an affine space of exact interpolators (with zero residual). The ridgeless estimator is the interpolator of minimal Euclidean norm:
  \[
    \min_{\eta\in\mathbb R^n} \tfrac12\|\eta\|^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad Z\eta=y.
  \]
  With Lagrange multiplier $\alpha\in\mathbb R^m$, the Lagrangian is $\mathcal L(\eta,\alpha)=\tfrac12\|\eta\|^2-\alpha^\top(Z\eta-y)$. The stationarity condition $\nabla_\eta\mathcal L=\eta-Z^\top\alpha=0$ requires $\eta=Z^\top\alpha$ (so $\eta\in\operatorname{range}Z^\top$). Substituting into the constraint gives $ZZ^\top\alpha=y$, so $\alpha=(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ and
  \[
    \hat\eta = Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y.
  \]
  Mapping to ambient coordinates gives
  \[
    \hat\theta = S\hat\eta = SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y \implies M = SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}.
  \]

  \item \textbf{Ambient bottleneck ($\gamma<1$, $\gamma\le\delta$, so $n_0\le\min\{m,n\}$):}
  Here the ambient dimension is the bottleneck. Assuming full rank, $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ has full column rank $n_0$ ($X^\top X$ is invertible) and $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ has full row rank $n_0$ ($SS^\top$ is invertible).
  The optimization problem decomposes into two stages via the ambient vector $\theta=S\eta\in\mathbb R^{n_0}$:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Outer least-squares in ambient space:} Because $S$ has full row rank, the map $S:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^{n_0}$ is surjective (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:saturation}), so $S\eta$ covers all of $\mathbb R^{n_0}$. The minimal least-squares loss $\min_\eta\|X(S\eta)-y\|$ equals the ambient OLS loss $\min_\theta\|X\theta-y\|$, which is uniquely minimized at the OLS solution
    \[
      \hat\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y.
    \]
    Because $X^\top X$ is invertible, $X$ is injective, so every least-squares minimizer $\eta$ must satisfy
    \[
      S\eta = \hat\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y.
    \]
    \item \emph{Inner minimum-norm selection in feature space:} Among all $\eta$ satisfying this underdetermined constraint $S\eta=\hat\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}}$, the minimum-norm choice $\hat\eta$ solves $\min_\eta\tfrac12\|\eta\|^2$ subject to $S\eta=\hat\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}}$. By the right-inverse formula (applied to $S$),
    \[
      \hat\eta = S^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}\hat\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}} = S^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y.
    \]
  \end{itemize}
  Mapping back to ambient space yields
  \[
    \hat\theta = S\hat\eta = SS^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y,
  \]
  since $SS^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}=I_{n_0}$. Thus $M = SS^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$. The random-feature map $S$ cancels out entirely, and $\hat\theta$ reduces to ordinary least squares in the original ambient coordinates.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Left inverse and its covariance]\label{lem:dd:leftInverse}
  \lean{Matrix.leftInverse_mul_self,
        Matrix.leftInverse_mul_transpose}
  \leanok
If $Z^\top Z$ is invertible, then $A:=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$ satisfies $AZ=I$ and $AA^\top=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$AZ=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}(Z^\top Z)=I$. Since $(Z^\top Z)^{-1}$ is symmetric,
$AA^\top=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top Z(Z^\top Z)^{-1}=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Right inverse; interpolation]\label{lem:dd:rightInverse}
  \lean{Matrix.self_mul_rightInverse,
        Matrix.rightInverse_interpolates}
  \leanok
If $ZZ^\top$ is invertible then $ZZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}=I$; consequently, for every target $y$ the coefficients
$\hat\eta=Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ interpolate: $Z\hat\eta=y$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$Z\bigl(Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}\bigr)=(ZZ^\top)(ZZ^\top)^{-1}=I$; apply it to $y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The right-inverse fit is the unique minimum-norm interpolator]\label{thm:dd:minNorm}
  \uses{lem:dd:rightInverse}
  \lean{Matrix.rightInverse_norm_sq_decomp,
        Matrix.eq_rightInverse_of_norm_le}
  \leanok
Let $ZZ^\top$ be invertible, $\hat\eta=Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$, and let $\eta$ satisfy $Z\eta=y$. Then
\[
  \|\eta\|^2=\|\hat\eta\|^2+\|\eta-\hat\eta\|^2 .
\]
In particular, an interpolator with $\|\eta\|\le\|\hat\eta\|$ equals $\hat\eta$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$\eta-\hat\eta\in\ker Z$ because both interpolate, while $\hat\eta=Z^\top w$ with $w=(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ lies in $\operatorname{range}Z^\top$.
For $u\in\ker Z$, $\langle u,Z^\top w\rangle=\langle Zu,w\rangle=0$, so $\eta-\hat\eta\perp\hat\eta$ and Pythagoras gives the
identity. If $\|\eta\|\le\|\hat\eta\|$ then $\|\eta-\hat\eta\|^2\le0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The left-inverse fit is the unique least-squares minimizer]\label{thm:dd:leastSquares}
  \uses{lem:dd:leftInverse, def:ntkcond:gramProjector, def:ntkgf:outputs}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.mseLoss_linear,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.norm_sq_residual_eq_leftInverse_add,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.norm_sq_residual_leftInverse,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.leftInverse_isMinOn,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.eq_leftInverse_of_norm_residual_le}
  \leanok
Let $Z^\top Z$ be invertible, $\hat\eta=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y$, and $P_Z=Z(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$ the Gram projector.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For the linear model $z\mapsto z\cdot\eta$ on the rows of $Z$, the dataset loss is
  $L(\eta)=\tfrac1{2m}\|Z\eta-y\|^2$ (Definition~\ref{def:ntkgf:outputs}).
  \item For every $\eta$, $\|Z\eta-y\|^2=\|Z\hat\eta-y\|^2+\|Z(\eta-\hat\eta)\|^2$, and $\|Z\hat\eta-y\|=\|(I-P_Z)y\|$.
  \item $\hat\eta$ is a global minimizer of $\|Z\eta-y\|$ and is the only one.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) is the definition of the mean-squared loss of a linear model. For (2), the normal equations
$Z^\top(Z\hat\eta-y)=0$ say that the residual $Z\hat\eta-y$ is orthogonal to $\operatorname{range}Z$, which contains
$Z(\eta-\hat\eta)$; expanding $Z\eta-y=(Z\hat\eta-y)+Z(\eta-\hat\eta)$ gives the Pythagoras identity, and
$Z\hat\eta-y=P_Zy-y=-(I-P_Z)y$. (3) follows from (2); uniqueness holds because equality forces $Z(\eta-\hat\eta)=0$, and $Z$ is
injective since $Z^\top Z$ is invertible.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Both ridgeless fits are minimum-norm least-squares estimators]\label{thm:dd:minNormLS}
  \uses{thm:dd:minNorm, thm:dd:leastSquares}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.leastSquaresLoss_eq_two_mul_card_mul_mseLoss,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.isMinOn_leastSquaresLoss_iff,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.isMinNormLeastSquares_of_norm_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.leftInverse_isMinNormLeastSquares,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.rightInverse_isMinNormLeastSquares}
  \leanok
Let $L(\eta)=\|y-Z\eta\|^2$ be the least-squares loss of \texttt{LinearRegression} and call $\hat\eta$ a \emph{minimum-norm least-squares
estimator} if it minimizes $L$ and has norm at most that of every other minimizer.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $L=2m\cdot\mathrm{mseLoss}$ of the linear model on the rows of $Z$, and $L$ has the same minimizers as $\eta\mapsto\|Z\eta-y\|$.
  \item If $Z^\top Z$ is invertible then $(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y$ is a minimum-norm least-squares estimator.
  \item If $ZZ^\top$ is invertible then $Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ is a minimum-norm least-squares estimator, with $L=0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) $L$ is the square of the residual norm, and $t\mapsto t^2$ is monotone on $[0,\infty)$; the factor $2m$ is the normalization $\tfrac1{2m}$ of the
dataset loss (Definition~\ref{def:ntkgf:outputs}). (2) By Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:leastSquares} the minimizer is unique, so it trivially has minimum norm.
(3) The estimator interpolates (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:rightInverse}), hence $L=0$ is the global minimum, and every minimizer is an interpolator,
whose norm is at least that of $Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNorm}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Context, foundations, and downstream role of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNormLS}]\label{rmk:dd:minNormLSContext}
  \uses{thm:dd:minNormLS}
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNormLS} provides the conceptual and algebraic foundation connecting the variational definition of ridgeless regression with the explicit operators analyzed in the rest of the chapter:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Context and variational role:}
  Definition~\ref{def:dd:randomFeatures} defines the feature coefficients $\hat\eta$ variationally as the minimum-norm minimizer among all minimizers of the least-squares loss $L(\eta)=\|y-Z\eta\|^2$. In general optimization, such two-tier minimization problems can be intricate. Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNormLS} establishes that whenever either Gram matrix ($Z^\top Z$ or $ZZ^\top$) is invertible, this variational problem has a closed-form solution given by the standard Moore--Penrose pseudoinverses: the left inverse $(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y$ in the overdetermined regime, and the right inverse $Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ in the underdetermined regime.

  \item \textbf{How it builds on earlier proofs:}
  The result synthesizes two complementary geometric properties proved immediately before it:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item When $Z^\top Z$ is invertible, Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:leastSquares} applies the Gram projector $P_Z=Z(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:leftInverse}) and the Pythagorean decomposition
    \[
      \|Z\eta-y\|^2 = \|Z\hat\eta-y\|^2 + \|Z(\eta-\hat\eta)\|^2
    \]
    to show that $\hat\eta=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y$ is the unique minimizer of the residual norm. Because the least-squares minimizer is unique, it is automatically the unique minimizer of smallest Euclidean norm.
    \item When $ZZ^\top$ is invertible, Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:rightInverse} shows that $\hat\eta=Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ interpolates the data ($Z\hat\eta=y$), achieving the global minimum loss $L=0$. Any other minimizer must also interpolate, meaning $\eta-\hat\eta\in\ker Z$. Since $\hat\eta\in\operatorname{range}Z^\top=(\ker Z)^\perp$, Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNorm} establishes the Pythagorean decomposition
    \[
      \|\eta\|^2 = \|\hat\eta\|^2 + \|\eta-\hat\eta\|^2,
    \]
    proving that $\hat\eta$ has strictly smaller norm than any other interpolator.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{How it is used by downstream results:}
  Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNormLS} guarantees that the explicit operators $M$ studied in subsequent sections correctly realize the ridgeless estimator:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Feature bottleneck (Section~\ref{sec:dd:feature}):}
    Justifies taking $\hat\eta=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y$ as the starting point for computing the variance trace $\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)=\operatorname{Tr}((Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:featureTrace}) and the resulting risk bounds.
    \item \emph{Ambient bottleneck (Section~\ref{sec:dd:ambient}):}
    Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:ambientOLS} combines both halves of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNormLS}: Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:leastSquares} applies to $X$ to fix the ambient predictions to the unique OLS fit $\hat\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}}$, while Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNorm} applies to $S$ to select the minimum-norm feature vector satisfying the interpolation constraint $S\eta=\hat\theta_{\mathrm{OLS}}$.
    \item \emph{Sample bottleneck (Section~\ref{sec:dd:sample}):}
    Guarantees that $\hat\eta=Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ is the minimum-norm interpolator (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleInterpolates}), from which the sample-bottleneck variance trace formula (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleVariance}) and bias bounds follow.
    \item \emph{Global double descent (Section~\ref{sec:dd:global}):}
    Provides the rigorous justification for the global piecewise definition of $M(X,S)$ in Definition~\ref{def:dd:minNormOperator} and Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:globalBranches}, ensuring that the conditional risk $\mathsf{cr}(\sigma^2,\theta,X,S)$ accurately tracks the true ridgeless random-feature regression risk across all non-singular asymptotic regimes.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}


\section{Feature bottleneck}\label{sec:dd:feature}

This regime is $\delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}$: fewer features than both samples and ambient dimensions.

\begin{lemma}[Variance trace reduction]\label{lem:dd:featureTrace}
  \uses{lem:dd:leftInverse}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_operator_transpose_mul_self_eq_featureBottleneck}
  \leanok
For $Z=XS$ with $Z^\top Z$ invertible and the operator $M=S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$,
\[
  \operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
With $A=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$ we have $M=SA$ and, by cyclic invariance of the trace and Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:leftInverse},
$\operatorname{Tr}(A^\top S^\top SA)=\operatorname{Tr}(S^\top S\,AA^\top)=\operatorname{Tr}(S^\top S(Z^\top Z)^{-1})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Algebraic core of the Wishart cancellation]\label{lem:dd:wishartCancel}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_smul_inv_mul_self,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_inverseWishartMean_mul_gram}
  \leanok
If $G\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ is invertible and $c\in\mathbb R$, then $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((cG^{-1})G\bigr)=cn$. In particular, for
$c=(m-n-1)^{-1}$ and $G=S^\top S$,
\[
  \operatorname{Tr}\Bigl(\tfrac1{m-n-1}(S^\top S)^{-1}\,S^\top S\Bigr)=\frac n{m-n-1}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$(cG^{-1})G=cI_n$, whose trace is $cn$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Role of Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:wishartCancel}]\label{rmk:dd:wishartRole}
  \uses{lem:dd:wishartCancel}
For a Gaussian design, conditionally on $S$ the matrix $(Z^\top Z)^{-1}$ is inverse-Wishart with mean
$(m-n-1)^{-1}(S^\top S)^{-1}$. The lemma shows that against $S^\top S$ this mean contributes the number
$n/(m-n-1)$. The Lean development does not use this conditional inverse-Wishart mean: the QR reduction (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceReduction}) removes the scale $S^\top S$ altogether, and the identity-scale mean
$\mathbb E\operatorname{Tr}(G^\top G)^{-1}=n/(m-n-1)$ is Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:invGramDiag}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Variance of the feature-bottleneck estimator]\label{thm:dd:featureVariance}
  \uses{thm:dd:linearBV, lem:dd:featureTrace}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.featureBottleneck_variance_eq_trace}
  \leanok
Let $Z=XS$ have $Z^\top Z$ invertible, $\hat\theta=S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top y$ with $y=X\theta_\star+\varepsilon$, and
$\varepsilon$ centered with covariance $\sigma^2I$. Then, with $\Sigma=I$,
\[
  V(\hat\theta)=\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$\hat\theta=My$ with $M=S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$. Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:linearBV} gives $V=\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}(MM^\top)$ and
$\operatorname{Tr}(MM^\top)=\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)$, which is Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:featureTrace}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotics of the feature-bottleneck variance and bias]\label{thm:dd:featureLimits}
  \uses{lem:dd:wishartCancel}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.featureBottleneck_asymptotic_variance_limit,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.featureBottleneck_asymptotic_bias_limit}
  \leanok
Let $m_k,n_k,n_{0,k}$ be sequences with $m_k\to\infty$, $n_k/m_k\to\delta\neq1$ and $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma\ne0$. Then
\[
  \sigma^2\frac{n_k}{m_k-n_k-1}\longrightarrow\sigma^2\frac{\delta}{1-\delta},\qquad
  \frac{\frac{n_{0,k}}{m_k}-\frac{n_k}{m_k}}{\frac{n_{0,k}}{m_k}\bigl(1-\frac{n_k}{m_k}\bigr)}\,\rho_\star
  \longrightarrow\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho_\star .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Write $n/(m-n-1)=(n/m)\big/\bigl(1-n/m-1/m\bigr)$. The numerator tends to $\delta$ and the denominator to $1-\delta\ne0$, so the
quotient tends to $\delta/(1-\delta)$ by continuity of division away from zero. The bias is a continuous function of
$(n_0/m,n/m)$ on $\{\gamma\neq0,\delta\ne1\}$.
\end{proof}


\section{Ambient bottleneck}\label{sec:dd:ambient}

This regime is $\gamma<1$, $\gamma\le\delta$: the ambient dimension is the bottleneck.

\begin{lemma}[Subspace saturation]\label{lem:dd:saturation}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.range_mulVecLin_mul_eq}
  \leanok
If $SS^\top$ is invertible (equivalently $S$ has full row rank, $n_0\le n$), then $\operatorname{range}(XS)=\operatorname{range}X$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \uses{lem:dd:rightInverse}
  \leanok
$\operatorname{range}(XS)\subseteq\operatorname{range}X$ trivially. Conversely, $S$ is surjective
(Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:rightInverse}), so for any $v$ pick $u$ with $Su=v$; then $Xv=(XS)u$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The ambient-bottleneck estimator is OLS]\label{thm:dd:ambientOLS}
  \uses{lem:dd:saturation, thm:dd:minNorm, thm:dd:leastSquares}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.mulVec_ambientBottleneck,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.ambientBottleneck_isMinOn,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.ambientBottleneck_norm_sq_decomp,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.eq_ambientBottleneck_of_norm_le}
  \leanok
Let $X^\top X$ and $SS^\top$ be invertible, $Z=XS$, and
$\hat\eta=S^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. Then $Z\hat\eta=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ is the OLS fit,
$\hat\eta$ minimizes $\|Z\eta-y\|$, and for every other minimizer $\eta$,
$\|\eta\|^2=\|\hat\eta\|^2+\|\eta-\hat\eta\|^2$. Hence $\hat\eta$ is the unique minimum-norm minimizer.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$Z\hat\eta=X\bigl(SS^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}\bigr)(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$, the OLS fit, which minimizes
$\|Xb-y\|$ over all $b$ and hence, by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:saturation}, minimizes $\|Z\eta-y\|$. Any other minimizer $\eta$ has
$S\eta$ equal to the OLS coefficients (injectivity of $X$, Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:leastSquares}), so $S\eta=S\hat\eta$ is an
interpolation constraint for $S$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:minNorm} applied to $S$ gives the decomposition.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Bias and variance of the ambient-bottleneck estimator]\label{thm:dd:ambientBV}
  \uses{thm:dd:linearBV, thm:dd:ambientOLS}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.ambientBottleneck_bias_variance}
  \leanok
With $X^\top X$, $SS^\top$ invertible, $\hat\theta=S\hat\eta$ is exactly unbiased and, for any weighting $\Sigma$ and isotropic noise,
\[
  B=0,\qquad V=R=\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((X^\top X)^{-1}\Sigma\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$\hat\theta=My$ with $M=S S^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$, so $MX=I$ and the bias term
$(MX-I)\theta_\star$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:linearBV} vanishes, while $MM^\top=(X^\top X)^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Asymptotics]\label{rmk:dd:ambientAsymptotics}
  \uses{thm:dd:ambientBV, thm:dd:featureLimits}
The expected variance $\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}((X^\top X)^{-1})=\sigma^2n_0/(m-n_0-1)$ tends to $\sigma^2\gamma/(1-\gamma)$; this is
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:featureLimits} with $n:=n_0$ and $\delta:=\gamma$, and is not duplicated in Lean. As in
Remark~\ref{rmk:dd:wishartRole}, the identification of $\operatorname{Tr}(X^\top X)^{-1}$ with $n_0/(m-n_0-1)$ is probabilistic.
\end{remark}


\section{Sample bottleneck}\label{sec:dd:sample}

This regime is $\gamma,\delta>1$: more ambient dimensions and features than samples. This section is deterministic; the convergence in probability of the variance trace to $(\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}$ is Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleVarianceProb}.

\begin{theorem}[The random-feature predictor interpolates the training data]\label{thm:dd:sampleInterpolates}
  \uses{lem:dd:rightInverse}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.sampleBottleneck_interpolates_training_targets}
  \leanok
If $Z=XS$ has full row rank ($ZZ^\top$ invertible) then $\hat\theta=SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ satisfies $X\hat\theta=y$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$X\hat\theta=(XS)Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y=Z\hat\eta=y$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:rightInverse}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Variance trace reduction]\label{lem:dd:sampleTrace}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_operator_transpose_mul_self_eq_sampleBottleneck}
  \leanok
For $M=SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$,
$\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((ZZ^\top)^{-1}\,Z S^\top S Z^\top\,(ZZ^\top)^{-1}\bigr)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Expand $M^\top M=(ZZ^\top)^{-1}Z\,S^\top S\,Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$ (the inverse is symmetric); no invertibility is needed for the
identity itself.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Variance of the sample-bottleneck estimator]\label{thm:dd:sampleVariance}
  \uses{thm:dd:linearBV, lem:dd:sampleTrace}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.sampleBottleneck_variance_eq_trace}
  \leanok
For $\hat\theta=SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}y$ and isotropic noise of variance $\sigma^2$,
$V(\hat\theta)=\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((ZZ^\top)^{-1}ZS^\top SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}\bigr)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:linearBV} with Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:sampleTrace}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Row-space and residual splitting of the sample-bottleneck operator]\label{lem:dd:sampleDecomp}
  \uses{lem:dd:sampleTrace, lem:dd:gramProjectorFacts}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.gramProjector_transpose_mul_operator,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.operator_eq_rowSpace_add_residual,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_operator_transpose_mul_self_eq_add,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_residual_operator_eq,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.operator_mul_design_sub_one_mulVec,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.sampleBottleneck_trace_eq_add}
  \leanok
Let $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ and $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ such that $XX^\top$ and $ZZ^\top$ are invertible ($Z=XS$), and let $P=P_X=X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}X=\operatorname{gramProjector} X^\top$. With $Z^\dagger=Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$ and $M=SZ^\dagger$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $PM=X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}$ and $M=X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}+(I-P)M$.
  \item The row-space and residual parts are orthogonal ($X(I-P)=0$), so
  \[
    \operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((XX^\top)^{-1}\bigr)+\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(((I-P)M)^\top((I-P)M)\bigr),
  \]
  where the residual trace equals $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((Z^\dagger(Z^\dagger)^\top)(S^\top(I-P)S)\bigr)$.
  \item For $y=X\theta$, the bias vector satisfies $(MX-I)\theta=(I-P)Sb-(I-P)\theta$ with $b=Z^\dagger X\theta$.
  \item The variance trace of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleVariance} is the sum of an ambient term and a residual term:
  \[
    \operatorname{Tr}\bigl((ZZ^\top)^{-1}ZS^\top SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}\bigr)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((XX^\top)^{-1}\bigr)+\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((Z^\dagger(Z^\dagger)^\top)\,S^\top(I-P)S\bigr).
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$XSZ^\dagger=ZZ^\dagger=I_m$, so $P_X M=X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}(X S Z^\dagger)=X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}$. The splitting is $M=PM+(I-P)M$, and orthogonality follows from $(X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1})^\top((I-P)M)=(XX^\top)^{-1}X(I-P)M=0$ since $X(I-P)=0$. The residual trace expands by cyclicity of trace and idempotence of $I-P$. For the bias, $(MX-I)=(I-P)(MX-I)$ because $PMX=X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}X=P$, giving $(I-P)Sb-(I-P)\theta$. Item~4 is item~2 with $\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)$ rewritten by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:sampleTrace}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Ratio limits beyond the threshold]\label{lem:dd:ratioLimits}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_atTop_of_tendsto_ratio_gt_one,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_ratio_div_sub_sub_one}
  \leanok
Let $b_k/m_k\to c>1$ and $m_k\to\infty$. Then $b_k\to\infty$ and $m_k/(b_k-m_k-1)\to(c-1)^{-1}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \uses{thm:dd:featureLimits}
  \leanok
Since $b_k/m_k\to c>1$, eventually $b_k>m_k\to\infty$. Writing $m/(b-m-1)=(m/b)/(1-m/b-1/b)$ with $m/b\to c^{-1}$ reduces to
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:featureLimits} with the roles of $n$ and $m$ exchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotics of the sample-bottleneck variance and bias]\label{thm:dd:sampleLimits}
  \uses{lem:dd:ratioLimits}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.sampleBottleneck_asymptotic_variance_limit,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.sampleBottleneck_asymptotic_bias_limit}
  \leanok
If $m_k\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>1$ and $n_k/m_k\to\delta>1$, then
\[
  \sigma^2\Bigl(\frac{m_k}{n_{0,k}-m_k-1}+\frac{m_k}{n_k-m_k-1}\Bigr)\longrightarrow\sigma^2\bigl((\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}\bigr).
\]
If only $\gamma\ne0$, $\delta\ne1$, then
$\bigl(1-\tfrac{m_k}{n_{0,k}}\bigr)\dfrac{n_k/m_k}{n_k/m_k-1}\rho_\star\to(1-\gamma^{-1})\dfrac{\delta}{\delta-1}\rho_\star$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
The variance limit is the sum of two applications of Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:ratioLimits} (with $b=n_0$ and $b=n$). The bias
expression is a continuous function of the two ratios away from $\gamma=0$, $\delta=1$.
\end{proof}


\section{The double-descent curve}\label{sec:dd:curve}

The three regimes assemble into one limiting risk curve in the width ratio $\delta$, for fixed $\gamma$. At the singular
values $\gamma=1$ or $\delta=1$ the third branch below has Mathlib's junk value $x/0=0$; no theorem evaluates there.

\begin{definition}[Asymptotic variance, bias and total risk]\label{def:dd:curve}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticVariance,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticBias,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticTotalRisk}
  \leanok
For $\sigma^2,\rho_\star,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb R$,
\[
  \mathsf V(\gamma,\delta)=\begin{cases}
  \sigma^2\dfrac{\delta}{1-\delta}&\delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}\\[1ex]
  \sigma^2\dfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}&\gamma<1,\ \gamma\le\delta\\[1ex]
  \sigma^2\bigl((\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}\bigr)&\text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}\qquad
  \mathsf B(\gamma,\delta)=\begin{cases}
  \dfrac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho_\star&\delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}\\[1ex]
  0&\gamma<1,\ \gamma\le\delta\\[1ex]
  (1-\gamma^{-1})\dfrac{\delta}{\delta-1}\rho_\star&\text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}
\]
and the total risk is $\mathsf R=\mathsf B+\mathsf V$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[The branches are the regime limits]\label{lem:dd:branches}
  \uses{def:dd:curve, thm:dd:featureLimits, thm:dd:ambientBV, thm:dd:sampleLimits}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticVariance_of_feature,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticVariance_of_ambient,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticVariance_of_sample,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticBias_of_feature,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticBias_of_ambient,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticBias_of_sample,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticTotalRisk_of_feature,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticTotalRisk_of_ambient,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticTotalRisk_of_sample}
  \leanok
On the feature regime $\delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}$, the ambient regime $\gamma<1\le\ldots$ ($\gamma<1$, $\gamma\le\delta$) and the sample
regime ($\gamma,\delta>1$) the curve $\mathsf V,\mathsf B,\mathsf R$ equals the closed forms of
Theorems~\ref{thm:dd:featureLimits}, \ref{thm:dd:ambientBV} and~\ref{thm:dd:sampleLimits}. Explicitly, for $\gamma,\delta>1$,
\[
  \mathsf R=\sigma^2(\gamma-1)^{-1}+(1-\gamma^{-1})\rho_\star+\kappa\,(\delta-1)^{-1},\qquad
  \kappa:=\sigma^2+(1-\gamma^{-1})\rho_\star .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Unfold the case distinction, using $\delta/(\delta-1)=1+(\delta-1)^{-1}$ for the bias
(\texttt{div\_sub\_one\_eq\_one\_add\_inv}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[One identity on both sides of the threshold]\label{lem:dd:keyIdentity}
  \uses{lem:dd:branches}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.div_sub_one_eq_one_add_inv,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.asymptoticTotalRisk_of_feature_of_one_lt,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.kappa_pos_of_nonneg}
  \leanok
Let $\gamma>1$ and $\kappa=\sigma^2+(1-\gamma^{-1})\rho_\star$. For $\delta<1$,
$\mathsf R=\kappa(1-\delta)^{-1}+(\gamma^{-1}\rho_\star-\sigma^2)$, and for $\delta>1$,
$\mathsf R=\kappa(\delta-1)^{-1}+\text{const}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:branches}): the \emph{same} coefficient $\kappa$ multiplies
$|1-\delta|^{-1}$ on both sides. Moreover $\kappa>0$ whenever $\sigma^2,\rho_\star\ge0$ are not both zero.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
For $\gamma>1$ and $\delta<1$ we have $\delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}$, so $\mathsf R=\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho_\star+\sigma^2\frac\delta{1-\delta}$.
Using $\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}=\gamma^{-1}+(1-\gamma^{-1})(1-\delta)^{-1}$ and $\frac\delta{1-\delta}=(1-\delta)^{-1}-1$
collects to the stated form. Positivity of $\kappa$ is immediate from $1-\gamma^{-1}>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Double descent for $\gamma>1$]\label{thm:dd:doubleDescent}
  \uses{lem:dd:keyIdentity}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.double_descent_first_ascent_strictMonoOn,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.double_descent_interpolation_divergence,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.double_descent_interpolation_divergence_of_nonneg,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.double_descent_second_descent_strictAntiOn,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.double_descent_second_descent_strictly_decreasing,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.double_descent_infinite_width_limit}
  \leanok
Let $\gamma>1$ and $\kappa=\sigma^2+(1-\gamma^{-1})\rho_\star>0$ (this holds if $\sigma^2,\rho_\star\ge0$, not both zero). Then the
limiting risk $\delta\mapsto\mathsf R(\gamma,\delta)$
\begin{enumerate}
  \item is strictly increasing on $\delta<1$ (first ascent);
  \item tends to $+\infty$ as $\delta\uparrow1$ (divergence at the interpolation threshold);
  \item is strictly decreasing on $\delta>1$ (second descent);
  \item tends to $\sigma^2/(\gamma-1)+(1-\gamma^{-1})\rho_\star$ as $\delta\to\infty$ (the risk of ridgeless regression on the raw
  inputs; no hypothesis on $\kappa$ is needed for this limit).
\end{enumerate}
The hypothesis $\kappa>0$ is sharp: without it, e.g.\ $\sigma^2=-1,\rho_\star=1$, the risk tends to $-\infty$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
By Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:keyIdentity}, on $\delta<1$ the risk is $\kappa(1-\delta)^{-1}+c_1$, which is strictly increasing and tends to
$+\infty$ as $\delta\uparrow1$ because $\kappa>0$ and $(1-\delta)^{-1}\to+\infty$. On $\delta>1$ it is $\kappa(\delta-1)^{-1}+c_2$,
strictly decreasing, and $(\delta-1)^{-1}\to0$ as $\delta\to\infty$ gives the limit $c_2=\sigma^2(\gamma-1)^{-1}+(1-\gamma^{-1})\rho_\star$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Flat segment and continuity for $\gamma<1$]\label{thm:dd:undercomplete}
  \uses{lem:dd:branches}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.undercomplete_saturated_flat_segment,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.undercomplete_continuousAt_gamma,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.undercomplete_continuous_transition_at_gamma}
  \leanok
Let $\gamma<1$. For every $\delta\ge\gamma$ the risk is constant, $\mathsf R(\gamma,\delta)=\sigma^2\gamma/(1-\gamma)$ (no benefit from extra
width once it reaches the ambient dimension). If $0<\gamma$, then $\delta\mapsto\mathsf R(\gamma,\delta)$ is continuous at $\delta=\gamma$, i.e.\
$\mathsf R(\gamma,\delta)\to\sigma^2\gamma/(1-\gamma)$ as $\delta\uparrow\gamma$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
The flat segment is the ambient branch (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:branches}). On $\delta<\gamma$ the feature branch is
$\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho_\star+\sigma^2\frac\delta{1-\delta}$, which is continuous at $\delta=\gamma$ with value
$0+\sigma^2\gamma/(1-\gamma)$; combining left and right pieces gives continuity.
\end{proof}


\section{Probabilistic background and the route taken}\label{sec:dd:route}

The deterministic layer reduces the risk of random-feature regression to traces of inverse Gram matrices. To turn it into a statement
about the \emph{random} estimator one needs four probabilistic facts about a Gaussian matrix $G\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$ with i.i.d.\
$\mathcal N(0,1)$ entries ($p\ge q$): (a) the law of $(G^\top G)^{-1}$ (or just of its trace), (b) invariance of the law under
orthogonal maps, (c) conditional independence of a Gaussian vector from the projector built from the others, (d) concentration in
probability. This section records the background that is already formalized (in Mathlib and in the NTK chapters), and then the route
by which the present development uses it. The route differs from the textbook route in six points, each of which removed a heavy
piece of random matrix theory from the formalization.

\subsection{Background that is already formalized}

The general Gaussian tools proved or imported for this chapter (Mathlib's standard Gaussian toolkit, the density of the standard Gaussian, radial and $\chi^2$ moments) are recorded in Chapter~\ref{chap:ntkf}, Section~\ref{sec:ntkf:radial}: Lemmas~\ref{lem:ntkf:mathlibGaussian} and~\ref{lem:ntkf:mathlibGamma}, Theorems~\ref{thm:ntkf:gaussianDensity} and~\ref{thm:ntkf:radialMoments}, Corollary~\ref{cor:ntkf:chiSquare}.




\begin{remark}[Background from the NTK chapters used by the route]\label{rmk:dd:ntkBackground}
  \uses{def:ntkcond:gramProjector, lem:ntkcond:gramProjectorProps, thm:ntkdeep:linearImage, lem:ntkr:matrixPush,
        lem:ntkcond:coordVec, thm:ntkcond:lemma226, thm:ntkcond:stdQuad, lem:ntkf:markov, lem:ntkf:chebyshev}
The following facts, already formalized and recorded in the NTK chapters, are the inputs for Section~\ref{sec:dd:gaussian} and the reductions of Section~\ref{sec:dd:gram}; none of them is re-proved here.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Gram projector} $P_\Phi=\Phi(\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top$ and its properties (Definition~\ref{def:ntkcond:gramProjector},
  Lemma~\ref{lem:ntkcond:gramProjectorProps}): an orthogonal projector for every $\Phi$, with $P\Phi=\Phi$ when $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is invertible.
  \item \emph{Linear images of Gaussians} (Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkdeep:linearImage}) and the pushforward of the standard Gaussian along a matrix
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:ntkr:matrixPush}): if $X$ has Gaussian rows and $Q^\top Q=I$ then $XQ$ again has i.i.d.\ $\mathcal N(0,1)$ entries, and
  $OS\overset d=S$ for orthogonal $O$.
  \item \emph{A Gaussian matrix is a standard Gaussian vector} (Lemma~\ref{lem:ntkcond:coordVec}), the bridge between matrix-valued and
  Euclidean-space statements.
  \item \emph{Independence of orthogonal components} (Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkcond:lemma226}): for a projector $P$ and Gaussian $W$, the images
  $WP$ and $W(I-P)$ are independent. This is what makes $\xi=X\theta_\perp$ independent of $G=XQ$ in the bias computation, and a column independent of the
  projector onto the other columns.
  \item \emph{Gaussian quadratic forms} (Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkcond:stdQuad}): $\mathbb E(z^\top Cz)^2=(\operatorname{Tr}C)^2+\operatorname{Tr}C^2+\|C\|_F^2$ for
  $z\sim\gamma$, hence $\operatorname{Var}(z^\top Cz)=2\operatorname{Tr}C^2$ for symmetric $C$.
  \item \emph{Markov and Chebyshev} in high-probability form (Lemmas~\ref{lem:ntkf:markov} and~\ref{lem:ntkf:chebyshev}).
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Alternatives that were tried or considered]\label{rmk:dd:alternatives}
  \uses{rmk:dd:route}
For the radial moments three routes were weighed. (i) Induction on $k$ with Fubini on $\mathbb R^k=\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^{k-1}$ needs a Gamma
convolution identity that Mathlib lacks. (ii) Gaussian integration by parts (Stein recursion) proves $\mathbb EY^{j+1}=(k+2j)\mathbb EY^j$ for integer $j\ge0$
only; the existing proof of this in the DeepLinear files is about six hundred lines and cannot produce $\mathbb EY^{-1}$ or $\mathbb EY^{-2}$, which the inverse-Gram
calculation requires. (iii) Polar coordinates against the density (Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkf:gaussianDensity}) turned out to be the shortest: the unit-ball volume
$\mu(B(0,1))$ and the Gaussian normalization constant enter through the single constant $K$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkf:radialMoments}, which is eliminated
by the normalization $\int1\,d\gamma_E=1$ and never evaluated. The density itself was obtained by comparing characteristic functions with
Mathlib's Gaussian Fourier integral (Lemma~\ref{lem:ntkf:mathlibGamma}) rather than by tensorizing one-dimensional densities.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Numerical sanity checks made before the reductions were proved]\label{rmk:dd:numerics}
  \uses{rmk:dd:route}
Before planning the reductions, the following were checked by simulation or exact linear algebra (these checks are not part of the formalization; the deterministic identities were afterwards proved in Lean, Section~\ref{sec:dd:gram}): the
trace identity of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceReduction} and the bias decomposition $\|(MX-I)\theta_\star\|^2=\|\theta_\perp\|^2+\|H^{-1}G^\top\xi\|^2$
agree to machine precision on random instances; the dual-vector formula for $((G^\top G)^{-1})_{jj}$ and the formula
$\operatorname{Tr}(W^{-2})=\sum_jW^{-1}_{jj}{}^2(1+\|b_j\|^2)$ ($b_j$ the regression coefficients of $g_j$ on the other columns) agree to machine precision; the Sherman--Morrison form
of $\|(I-P_S)e_1\|^2$ agrees; and Monte Carlo means match $\mathbb E\operatorname{Tr}W^{-1}=q/(p-q-1)$ and
$\mathbb E\operatorname{Tr}W^{-2}=q(p-1)/((p-q)(p-q-1)(p-q-3))$ to within sampling error. For the sample regime the Monte Carlo mean of
$\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)$ matches $m/(n_0-m-1)+m/(n-m-1)$ to about one percent for three shapes; the in-probability limit $(\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleVarianceProb} is the limit of this expression.
\end{remark}


\section{Chi-squared tail bounds: inputs for the probabilistic layer}\label{sec:dd:moments}

For a Gaussian matrix $G\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$ ($p\ge q$) the diagonal entries of $(G^\top G)^{-1}$ are
$1/\|(I-P_{-j})g_j\|^2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:dualVector}), and $\|(I-P_{-j})g_j\|^2$ is a $\chi^2$ variable (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:invGramDiag}). The moment calculus of
$\chi^2$ variables needed for this (the radial moment formula, the moments $\mathbb EY^j$ and the inverse moments $\mathbb EY^{-1},\mathbb EY^{-2}$) is
general Gaussian machinery and is recorded in Chapter~\ref{chap:ntkf}, Section~\ref{sec:ntkf:radial}
(Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkf:radialMoments}, Corollary~\ref{cor:ntkf:chiSquare}). The fourth-moment tail bound below is the double-descent-specific
consequence, in \texttt{ForMathlib/Probability/StdGaussianChiSqTails.lean}.

\begin{theorem}[Fourth central moment and tail bound of $\chi^2_k$]\label{thm:dd:chiTail}
  \uses{cor:ntkf:chiSquare}
  \lean{ProbabilityTheory.integrable_norm_sq_sub_pow_four,
        ProbabilityTheory.integral_norm_sq_sub_pow_four,
        ProbabilityTheory.measureReal_norm_sq_sub_abs_ge_le,
        ProbabilityTheory.measureReal_norm_sq_sub_ge_le,
        ProbabilityTheory.measure_dev_le_of_map_norm_sq}
  \leanok
Let $\dim E=k\ge1$ and $x\sim\gamma_E$. Then $\mathbb E\bigl(\|x\|^2-k\bigr)^4=12k^2+48k$, for every $t>0$ $\mathbb P(|\|x\|^2-k|\ge t)\le(12k^2+48k)/t^4$, and for every $\varepsilon>0$,
\[
  \mathbb P\bigl(\bigl|\|x\|^2-k\bigr|\ge\varepsilon k\bigr)\le\frac{60}{\varepsilon^4k^2}.
\]
The last bound holds verbatim for \emph{any} random variable $f$ on any probability space whose law is that of $\|x\|^2$, $x\sim\gamma_{\mathbb R^k}$; this is the form applied to $\|Pg\|^2$, $\|g\|^2$ and $1/((W^\top W)^{-1})_{jj}$ below.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Expand $(Y-k)^4=Y^4-4kY^3+6k^2Y^2-4k^3Y+k^4$ and insert the moments of Corollary~\ref{cor:ntkf:chiSquare}(1) for $j\le4$:
$k(k+2)(k+4)(k+6)-4k^2(k+2)(k+4)+6k^3(k+2)-4k^4+k^4=12k^2+48k$. Markov's inequality for $(Y-k)^4$ at level $(\varepsilon k)^4$ bounds the
probability by $(12k^2+48k)/(\varepsilon k)^4\le60/(\varepsilon^4k^2)$, since $k\ge1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why a fourth moment]\label{rmk:dd:whyFourth}
  \uses{thm:dd:chiTail}
Summed over the $q$ diagonal entries of $(G^\top G)^{-1}$ by a union bound, this tail costs $60\,q/(\varepsilon^4\nu^2)$ with $\nu=p-q+1$,
which tends to $0$ when $q/p\to\rho<1$. A second-moment (Chebyshev) bound would only give $O(q/\nu)=O(1)$ and would not suffice. This route
needs only the \emph{marginal} laws of the diagonal entries, not the covariances between them.
\end{remark}


\section{Deterministic reductions for Gaussian inputs}\label{sec:dd:gram}

Nothing in this section is probabilistic. It contains the linear algebra that reduces the two random quantities of the feature-bottleneck estimator
$\hat\theta=My$, $M=S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$, $Z=XS$, namely $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S\bigr)$ and $\|(MX-I)\theta_\star\|^2$,
to statements about a matrix $G=XQ$ that is again standard Gaussian when $X$ is (Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkdeep:linearImage}) and $Q^\top Q=I$. The
reductions are exact identities for every full-column-rank $S$, so they hold on the whole probability space, not only almost surely.
In Lean they live in \texttt{DoubleDescent/GramInverse.lean}; index types are arbitrary finite types.

\begin{lemma}[Orthonormal frame of a full-column-rank matrix]\label{lem:dd:orthFactor}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.exists_orthonormal_factor}
  \leanok
If $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ has $S^\top S$ invertible, then $S=QR$ with $Q^\top Q=I_n$ and $R$ invertible.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Take $R=\sqrt{S^\top S}$ (the positive semidefinite square root, symmetric with $RR=S^\top S$), invertible because $\det(R)^2=\det(S^\top S)\neq0$,
and $Q=SR^{-1}$. Then $Q^\top Q=R^{-1}S^\top SR^{-1}=R^{-1}R^2R^{-1}=I$. No Gram--Schmidt and no triangularity are needed, since only
$R^\top R=S^\top S$ is used downstream.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Full column rank and deleted columns]\label{lem:dd:fullRank}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.isUnit_det_gram_iff_injective,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.isUnit_det_gram_submatrix_ne}
  \leanok
$Z^\top Z$ is invertible if and only if $Z$ has trivial kernel. If so, then the matrix $Z_{-j}$ obtained by deleting column $j$ also has
$Z_{-j}^\top Z_{-j}$ invertible.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
If $Z^\top Zv=0$ then $\|Zv\|^2=v^\top Z^\top Zv=0$, so $Zv=0$; conversely a kernel-free $Z$ has positive definite Gram matrix. For the second statement, a vector
$v$ in the kernel of $Z_{-j}$ extends by $0$ in coordinate $j$ to a vector $\tilde v$ with $Z\tilde v=Z_{-j}v=0$, so $\tilde v=0$ and $v=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Further facts on the Gram projector]\label{lem:dd:gramProjectorFacts}
  \uses{def:ntkcond:gramProjector, lem:ntkcond:gramProjectorProps}
  \lean{Matrix.one_sub_gramProjector_mulVec,
        Matrix.trace_gramProjector,
        Matrix.dotProduct_one_sub_gramProjector_mulVec_self,
        Matrix.gramProjector_submatrix_equiv,
        Matrix.gramProjector_orthonormal_mul}
  \leanok
Let $\Phi\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}$ and $P_\Phi=\Phi(\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top$. (1) $(I-P_\Phi)y=y-\Phi(\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$, the least-squares residual. If $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is invertible then (2) $\operatorname{Tr}P_\Phi=\#\text{columns}$ and
(3) $\|(I-P_\Phi)y\|^2=\|y\|^2-y^\top P_\Phi y$. (4) $P_\Phi$ is unchanged when the columns are relabelled by a bijection. (5) If $U^\top U=I$ then $P_{U\Phi}=UP_\Phi U^\top$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) is the definition with $\Phi(\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top y=P_\Phi y$. (2) $\operatorname{Tr}(\Phi(\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top)=\operatorname{Tr}((\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top\Phi)=\operatorname{Tr}I_m$ by cyclicity. (3) $I-P_\Phi$ is a symmetric idempotent, so
$\|(I-P_\Phi)y\|^2=y^\top(I-P_\Phi)y$. (4) A relabelling conjugates $\Phi^\top\Phi$ by a permutation and the sum over columns is reindexed. (5) $(U\Phi)^\top(U\Phi)=\Phi^\top\Phi$, so only the outer factors change.
These are the deterministic ingredients for deleting a column (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:dualVector}) and for the law of $\|(I-P_{-j})w_j\|^2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:invGramDiag}), whose trace is $p-q$ by (2).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[QR trace reduction]\label{thm:dd:traceReduction}
  \uses{lem:dd:orthFactor, lem:dd:wishartCancel}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_inv_gram_mul_gram_of_factor}
  \leanok
Let $S=QR$ with $Q^\top Q=I_n$ and $R$ invertible, $G=XQ$ and $Z=XS=GR$. If $G^\top G$ is invertible, then
\[
  \operatorname{Tr}\bigl((Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S\bigr)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((G^\top G)^{-1}\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$Z^\top Z=R^\top(G^\top G)R$ and $S^\top S=R^\top R$, so $(Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S=R^{-1}(G^\top G)^{-1}R$, whose trace is
$\operatorname{Tr}(G^\top G)^{-1}$ by cyclicity. The feature covariance $S^\top S$ cancels, so only identity-covariance Gaussian matrices ever occur.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Dual-vector formula for the inverse Gram matrix]\label{thm:dd:dualVector}
  \uses{lem:dd:fullRank, lem:dd:leftInverse, lem:dd:gramProjectorFacts, def:ntkcond:gramProjector, lem:ntkcond:gramProjectorProps}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.inv_gram_apply_self,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.inv_gram_apply_ne}
  \leanok
Let $Z^\top Z$ be invertible, $z_j$ the $j$th column of $Z$, $Z_{-j}$ the matrix without it, $P_{-j}$ its Gram projector, and
$b_j=(Z_{-j}^\top Z_{-j})^{-1}Z_{-j}^\top z_j$ the least-squares coefficients of $z_j$ on the other columns. Then
\[
  \bigl((Z^\top Z)^{-1}\bigr)_{jj}=\frac1{\|(I-P_{-j})z_j\|^2},\qquad
  \bigl((Z^\top Z)^{-1}\bigr)_{ij}=-b_{j,i}\,\bigl((Z^\top Z)^{-1}\bigr)_{jj}\quad(i\neq j).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Put $r=z_j-Z_{-j}b_j=(I-P_{-j})z_j$. The normal equations for $Z_{-j}$ give $Z_{-j}^\top r=0$, and $z_j=r+Z_{-j}b_j$ gives $z_j^\top r=\|r\|^2$, so
$Z^\top r=\|r\|^2e_j$. Moreover $r=Zc$ for $c=e_j-(b_j\text{ extended by }0)$. Hence
$Z^\top Zc=\|r\|^2e_j$ and, applying $(Z^\top Z)^{-1}$, $c=\|r\|^2(Z^\top Z)^{-1}e_j$. Reading coordinate $j$ (where $c_j=1$) gives the first identity and
coordinate $i\neq j$ (where $c_i=-b_{j,i}$) the second.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[$\operatorname{Tr}W^{-2}$ through the column regressions]\label{thm:dd:traceSecond}
  \uses{thm:dd:dualVector}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_inv_gram_mul_self}
  \leanok
If $W=Z^\top Z$ is invertible, then
\[
  \operatorname{Tr}(W^{-1}W^{-1})=\sum_jW^{-1}_{jj}{}^2\bigl(1+\|b_j\|^2\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$W^{-1}$ is symmetric, so $\operatorname{Tr}W^{-2}=\sum_j\sum_i(W^{-1}_{ij})^2$. Split off $i=j$ and use $W^{-1}_{ij}=-b_{j,i}W^{-1}_{jj}$
from Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:dualVector} for $i\neq j$. By the same theorem, $W^{-1}_{jj}$ depends on $z_j$ only through the residual
$(I-P_{-j})z_j$ while $b_j$ depends on $P_{-j}z_j$; for Gaussian $Z$ these are independent, which is what makes $\mathbb E\operatorname{Tr}W^{-2}$ computable.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Bias reduction (Pythagoras)]\label{thm:dd:biasReduction}
  \uses{lem:dd:orthFactor, thm:dd:traceReduction, def:dd:linearModel, thm:dd:linearBV, lem:dd:leftInverse}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.operator_eq_orthonormal_frame,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.bias_eq_omitted_add_fit_error}
  \leanok
Let $S=QR$ with $Q^\top Q=I_n$ and $R$ invertible, $P_S=QQ^\top$, $G=XQ$ with $H=G^\top G$ invertible, $Z=XS$ and $M=S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$.
Then $M=QH^{-1}G^\top$ (the triangular factor $R$ cancels), and for $\theta_\perp=(I-P_S)\theta_\star$ and $\xi=X\theta_\perp$,
\[
  \|(MX-I)\theta_\star\|^2=\|\theta_\perp\|^2+\|H^{-1}G^\top\xi\|^2 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
$Z^\top Z=R^\top HR$, so $M=QR\,R^{-1}H^{-1}R^{-\top}R^\top G^\top=QH^{-1}G^\top$ and $MG=Q$. Decompose $\theta_\star=QQ^\top\theta_\star+\theta_\perp$; then
$MXQQ^\top\theta_\star=MGQ^\top\theta_\star=QQ^\top\theta_\star$, so $(MX-I)\theta_\star=MX\theta_\perp-\theta_\perp=Qw-\theta_\perp$ with $w=H^{-1}G^\top\xi$.
Since $Q^\top\theta_\perp=0$, the vector $Qw$ is orthogonal to $\theta_\perp$, and $\|Qw\|=\|w\|$ because $Q^\top Q=I$; Pythagoras gives the identity.
The first term is the omitted signal and the second the error from fitting the noise-like $\xi$, which for Gaussian $X$ is independent of $G$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkcond:lemma226}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Invariance and independence}

\begin{lemma}[Orthogonal invariance of Gaussian matrices]\label{lem:dd:invariance}
  \uses{thm:ntkdeep:linearImage, lem:ntkr:matrixPush}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_pi_gaussianReal_mulVec,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_gaussianMatrix_mul_orthonormal,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_gaussianMatrix_transpose,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_gaussianMatrix_orthogonal_mul}
  \leanok
Let $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$ be Gaussian.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $AA^\top=I$ and $g$ is a Gaussian vector then $Ag$ is a Gaussian vector.
  \item If $Q^\top Q=I$ then $WQ$ is again Gaussian (right invariance; $Q$ may have fewer columns than $W$).
  \item $W^\top$ is Gaussian, and if $U^\top U=I$ then $UW$ is Gaussian (left invariance).
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) is the pushforward of the standard Gaussian along a matrix with orthonormal rows (Lemma~\ref{lem:ntkr:matrixPush} and Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkdeep:linearImage}).
(2) applies (1) to each row of $W$ with $A=Q^\top$. (3) The transpose is a coordinate permutation of the standard Gaussian on the pair-indexed vector of entries, which
preserves the product measure; $UW=(W^\top U^\top)^\top$ then follows from (2) conjugated by the transpose.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Orthogonal matrices are transitive on spheres]\label{lem:dd:householder}
  \lean{Matrix.exists_orthogonal_mulVec_eq}
  \leanok
If $x^\top x=y^\top y$ there is $U$ with $UU^\top=U^\top U=I$ and $Ux=y$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
If $x=y$ take $U=I$. Otherwise the Householder reflection $U=I-2ww^\top/\|w\|^2$ with $w=x-y$ is orthogonal and swaps $x$ and $y$, because
$\|x\|=\|y\|$ gives $2w^\top x=\|w\|^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Projected Gaussian norms are chi-squared]\label{lem:dd:projectedChi}
  \uses{thm:dd:chiTail, cor:ntkf:chiSquare, lem:ntkcond:gramProjectorProps}
  \lean{Matrix.orthogonalDecomposition,
        Matrix.exists_orthonormal_rows_of_isStarProjection,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_dotProduct_projector_mulVec,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_projector_norm_sq_dev_le}
  \leanok
Let $g$ be a Gaussian vector and $P$ an orthogonal projection matrix ($P^\top=P=P^2$) of trace $r\ge1$. Then $\|Pg\|^2\sim\chi^2_r$, and for every $\varepsilon>0$
\[
  \mathbb P\bigl(\bigl|\|Pg\|^2-r\bigr|\ge\varepsilon r\bigr)\le\frac{60}{\varepsilon^4r^2}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
A projection of trace $r$ factors as $P=A^\top A$ with $A\in\mathbb R^{r\times p}$ having orthonormal rows, so $\|Pg\|^2=\|Ag\|^2$ and $Ag$ is a standard Gaussian
in $\mathbb R^r$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:invariance}(1). The tail bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:chiTail} transported along the equality of laws.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Independent column; conditional law from a fibrewise law]\label{lem:dd:columnSplit}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measurePreserving_columnSplit,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_prod_eq_of_ae}
  \leanok
(1) For Gaussian $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times(q+1)}$, the map $W\mapsto(\text{column }j,\ \text{the other columns})$ is measure preserving into the product of a Gaussian vector and a
Gaussian $p\times q$ matrix; in particular column $j$ is independent of the rest. (2) Let $F:\alpha\times\beta\to\gamma$ be measurable and suppose that for $B$-a.e.\ $b$ the law of
$a\mapsto F(a,b)$ under $A$ is a fixed measure $\lambda$. Then the law of $F$ under $A\otimes B$ is $\lambda$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) is the reindexing of the pair-indexed Gaussian vector along $\{j\}\sqcup\{j\}^c$, which is measure preserving for the product measure. (2) is Fubini: for a measurable set $T$,
$(A\otimes B)(F^{-1}T)=\int A\{a:F(a,b)\in T\}\,dB(b)=\lambda(T)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Full rank and the inverse Gram matrix}

\begin{theorem}[Gaussian matrices have full column rank]\label{thm:dd:fullRankAS}
  \uses{lem:dd:columnSplit, lem:dd:fullRank, thm:ntkf:subspaceNull}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.injective_mulVec_cons,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.ae_isUnit_det_gram_gaussianMatrix}
  \leanok
For $q\le p$, almost every Gaussian $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$ has $W^\top W$ invertible.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Induction on the number of columns. Split off column $0$ from the remaining $q$ columns, which are independent of it (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:columnSplit}(1)). By induction the remaining columns a.s.\ have trivial kernel, so their
span is a subspace of dimension $q<p$, a proper subspace of $\mathbb R^p$. A proper subspace is null for the Gaussian (Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkf:subspaceNull}: the Gaussian has a Lebesgue density and proper subspaces are Lebesgue-null). Hence
column $0$ a.s.\ avoids the span, the kernel of the full matrix is trivial, and $W^\top W$ is invertible by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:fullRank}. No polynomial zero-set argument is used.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Law of the diagonal of the inverse Gram matrix]\label{thm:dd:invGramDiag}
  \uses{thm:dd:dualVector, thm:dd:fullRankAS, lem:dd:columnSplit, lem:dd:projectedChi, lem:dd:gramProjectorFacts, cor:ntkf:chiSquare}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.inv_gram_apply_self_succAbove,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_inv_gram_diag_gaussianMatrix,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.integral_inv_gram_diag_gaussianMatrix,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.integral_trace_inv_gram_gaussianMatrix}
  \leanok
Let $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times(q+1)}$ be Gaussian with $q+1\le p$. For every $j$,
\[
  \bigl((W^\top W)^{-1}\bigr)_{jj}\ \overset d=\ \frac1{\chi^2_{p-q}}\qquad(p-q=p-(q+1)+1).
\]
Consequently $\mathbb E\bigl((W^\top W)^{-1}\bigr)_{jj}=1/(p-q-2)$ for $q+2<p$, and for a Gaussian $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$ with $q+1<p$,
$\mathbb E\operatorname{Tr}(W^\top W)^{-1}=q/(p-q-1)$, the inverse-Wishart mean.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
By Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:dualVector}, $((W^\top W)^{-1})_{jj}=1/\|(I-P_{-j})w_j\|^2$ whenever $W^\top W$ is invertible, which holds a.s.\ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:fullRankAS}).
For fixed other columns, $I-P_{-j}$ is an orthogonal projection of trace $p-q$, so $\|(I-P_{-j})w_j\|^2\sim\chi^2_{p-q}$ for the independent Gaussian column $w_j$
(Lemmas~\ref{lem:dd:projectedChi} and~\ref{lem:dd:columnSplit}(1)); by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:columnSplit}(2) this is also the law under the product measure. The means are
$\mathbb E[1/\chi^2_\nu]=1/(\nu-2)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:ntkf:chiSquare}) and linearity of the trace. The Wishart density, the Bartlett decomposition and Haar measure are never used.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Concentration of the inverse Gram trace]\label{thm:dd:traceConcentration}
  \uses{thm:dd:invGramDiag, thm:dd:chiTail}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measureReal_inv_gram_diag_deviation_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_trace_inv_gram_deviation_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_trace_inv_gram_deviation}
  \leanok
Let $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times m}$ be Gaussian with $1\le m\le p$, $\nu=p-m+1$ and $0<\varepsilon<1$. Then
\[
  \mathbb P\Bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(W^\top W)^{-1}\notin\Bigl[\tfrac m{\nu(1+\varepsilon)},\tfrac m{\nu(1-\varepsilon)}\Bigr]\Bigr)\le\frac{60\,m}{\varepsilon^4\nu^2}.
\]
Moreover, if $1\le m_k\le p_k$, $p_k\to\infty$ and $m_k/p_k\to\rho<1$, then $\operatorname{Tr}(W_k^\top W_k)^{-1}\to\rho/(1-\rho)$ in probability.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
For each diagonal entry, Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:invGramDiag} and the tail bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:chiTail} give
$\mathbb P(|1/((W^\top W)^{-1})_{jj}-\nu|\ge\varepsilon\nu)\le60/(\varepsilon^4\nu^2)$. A union bound over $j\le m$ costs $60m/(\varepsilon^4\nu^2)$; off the bad event
$\operatorname{Tr}(W^\top W)^{-1}=\sum_j((W^\top W)^{-1})_{jj}$ lies in the stated interval. For the limit, $m_k/\nu_k\to\rho/(1-\rho)$, so for a fixed small tolerance the interval lies within $\varepsilon$ of the limit
eventually, and $60m_k/(\varepsilon^4\nu_k^2)\to0$ since $\nu_k\to\infty$ and $m_k=O(\nu_k)$. Only the marginal laws of the diagonal entries are used, never covariances between them.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Concentration of a weighted inverse Gram trace]\label{thm:dd:weightedTraceConcentration}
  \uses{thm:dd:traceConcentration, lem:dd:invariance}
  \lean{Matrix.exists_trace_mul_eq_sum_eigenvalues,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measureReal_weighted_diag_inv_gram_deviation_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_trace_mul_inv_gram_deviation_le}
  \leanok
Let $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times(q+1)}$ be Gaussian with $q+1\le p$, and let $C\in\mathbb R^{(q+1)\times(q+1)}$ be positive semidefinite. For every $0<\varepsilon<1$, with $\nu=p-q$:
\[
  \mathbb P\Bigl(\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(C(W^\top W)^{-1}\bigr)\notin\Bigl[\tfrac{\operatorname{Tr} C}{\nu(1+\varepsilon)},\tfrac{\operatorname{Tr} C}{\nu(1-\varepsilon)}\Bigr]\Bigr)\le\frac{60(q+1)}{\varepsilon^4\nu^2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
By orthogonal diagonalization (\texttt{Matrix.exists\_trace\_mul\_eq\_sum\_eigenvalues}), $C=U\operatorname{diag}(w)U^\top$ with orthogonal $U$ and $w_j\ge0$, so $\operatorname{Tr}(C(W^\top W)^{-1})=\sum_j w_j(U^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}U)_{jj}=\sum_j w_j((WU)^\top(WU))^{-1}_{jj}$. Since $WU$ is Gaussian by orthogonal invariance, each diagonal entry concentrates as in Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceConcentration}, and the convex combination with $\sum w_j=\operatorname{Tr} C$ preserves the bounds.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Quadratic forms and the fit error}

\begin{theorem}[The Rayleigh quotient of the Gram inverse is exactly chi-squared]\label{thm:dd:quadFormLaw}
  \uses{thm:dd:invGramDiag, lem:dd:invariance, lem:dd:householder, lem:dd:columnSplit}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_quadForm_inv_gram,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.ae_dotProduct_self_ne_zero_pi_gaussianReal,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.map_dotProduct_div_quadForm_inv_gram}
  \leanok
Let $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times(q+1)}$ be Gaussian with $q+1\le p$. (1) For every fixed unit vector $u$, $u^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}u\overset d=1/\chi^2_{p-q}$.
(2) For an independent Gaussian vector $z\in\mathbb R^{q+1}$,
\[
  \frac{\|z\|^2}{z^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}z}\ \sim\ \chi^2_{p-q}\quad\text{exactly.}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) By Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:householder} choose an orthogonal $U$ with $Ue_0=u$. Then $u^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}u=e_0^\top(U^\top W^\top WU)^{-1}e_0=((\tilde W^\top\tilde W)^{-1})_{00}$ with $\tilde W=WU$, which is Gaussian
by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:invariance}; apply the diagonal law of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:invGramDiag}. (2) For fixed $z\neq0$ (almost sure) apply (1) to $u=z/\|z\|$, giving that the quotient has law $\chi^2_{p-q}$ for \emph{every} such $z$; Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:columnSplit}(2)
assembles the product law with no independence or polar decomposition of $z$ needed.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Concentration of a Gaussian quadratic form in the Gram inverse]\label{thm:dd:quadFormConc}
  \uses{thm:dd:quadFormLaw, thm:dd:chiTail}
  \lean{ProbabilityTheory.measure_dev_le_of_map_norm_sq,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_quadForm_inv_gram_deviation_le}
  \leanok
With $W,z$ as in Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:quadFormLaw}, $\nu=p-q$, $n=q+1$ and $0<\eta<1$, the quadratic form $z^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}z$ lies in
$\bigl[\tfrac{n(1-\eta)}{\nu(1+\eta)},\tfrac{n(1+\eta)}{\nu(1-\eta)}\bigr]$ except on an event of probability at most $60/(\eta^4\nu^2)+60/(\eta^4n^2)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Both $\|z\|^2\sim\chi^2_n$ and $\|z\|^2/z^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}z\sim\chi^2_\nu$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:quadFormLaw}(2)); the law-transfer form of the $\chi^2$ tail
(\texttt{measure\_dev\_le\_of\_map\_norm\_sq}: the bound holds for any random variable having the law of $\|x\|^2$) bounds each deviation. Off both events the quotient of the two is sandwiched as stated.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic form of the Gram inverse in a fixed direction]\label{thm:dd:quadFormFixed}
  \uses{thm:dd:quadFormLaw, thm:dd:chiTail}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_quadForm_inv_gram_fixed_mem_le}
  \leanok
Let $W\in\mathbb R^{p\times(q+1)}$ be Gaussian with $q+1\le p$, $\nu=p-q$, $0<\eta<1$, and let $\xi\in\mathbb R^{q+1}$ be a \emph{fixed} vector. Then
\[
  \mathbb P\Bigl(\xi^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}\xi\notin\Bigl[\tfrac{\|\xi\|^2}{\nu(1+\eta)},\tfrac{\|\xi\|^2}{\nu(1-\eta)}\Bigr]\Bigr)\le\frac{60}{\eta^4\nu^2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
For $\xi=0$ the event is empty. Otherwise $\xi^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}\xi=\|\xi\|^2\,u^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}u$ with the unit vector $u=\xi/\|\xi\|$, and $u^\top(W^\top W)^{-1}u\overset d=1/\chi^2_\nu$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:quadFormLaw}(1). The fourth-moment tail of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:chiTail} bounds
$\mathbb P(|\chi^2_\nu-\nu|\ge\eta\nu)$ by $60/(\eta^4\nu^2)$, and off this event $1/\chi^2_\nu\in[\frac1{\nu(1+\eta)},\frac1{\nu(1-\eta)}]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Fit error of a Gaussian regression]\label{thm:dd:fitError}
  \uses{thm:dd:quadFormConc, lem:dd:orthFactor, lem:dd:invariance}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.fitError_eq_quadForm,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_fitError_deviation_le}
  \leanok
Let $G\in\mathbb R^{m\times(q+1)}$ be Gaussian with $q+1\le m$ and $\zeta\in\mathbb R^m$ an independent Gaussian vector. With $\nu=m-q$, $n=q+1$ and $0<\eta<1$, the squared fit error
$\|(G^\top G)^{-1}G^\top\zeta\|^2$ lies in $\bigl[\tfrac{n(1-\eta)}{\nu(1+\eta)},\tfrac{n(1+\eta)}{\nu(1-\eta)}\bigr]$ except on an event of probability at most
$60/(\eta^4\nu^2)+60/(\eta^4n^2)$. It concentrates at $n/(m-n+1)$ without any second moment of the inverse Wishart matrix.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
If $G=QR$ with $Q^\top Q=I$ and $R$ symmetric invertible, then $(G^\top G)^{-1}G^\top\zeta=R^{-1}Q^\top\zeta$ and so $\|(G^\top G)^{-1}G^\top\zeta\|^2=z^\top(G^\top G)^{-1}z$ with $z=Q^\top\zeta$
(\texttt{fitError\_eq\_quadForm}). For a fixed full-rank $G$ (a.s.\ by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:fullRankAS}, with $Q$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:orthFactor}), $z$ is a standard Gaussian by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:invariance}(1), so the bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:quadFormConc}
applies, and Fubini over $G$ finishes.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Bias of a Gaussian subspace}

\begin{theorem}[Omitted signal of a Gaussian random subspace]\label{thm:dd:projectorProbability}
  \uses{lem:dd:invariance, lem:dd:householder, lem:dd:projectedChi, thm:dd:chiTail, def:ntkcond:gramProjector}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_resid_deviation_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_resid_deviation}
  \leanok
Let $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ be Gaussian, $1\le n\le n_0$, $P_S$ the Gram projector of $S$, $0<\varepsilon\le\tfrac12$. For every $\theta\in\mathbb R^{n_0}$,
\[
  \mathbb P\Bigl(\bigl|\|(I-P_S)\theta\|^2-(1-\tfrac n{n_0})\|\theta\|^2\bigr|>4\varepsilon\|\theta\|^2\Bigr)\le\frac{60}{\varepsilon^4n^2}+\frac{60}{\varepsilon^4n_0^2}.
\]
Consequently, if $n_k\le n_{0,k}$, $n_k\to\infty$ and $\|\theta_k\|^2\le M$, then $\|(I-P_{S_k})\theta_k\|^2-(1-n_k/n_{0,k})\|\theta_k\|^2\to0$ in probability; only $n_k\to\infty$ is needed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
By left invariance of $S$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:invariance}(3)) and transitivity of the orthogonal group on spheres (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:householder}) the failure probability is the same for all $\theta$ of the same norm,
and by homogeneity for all $\theta\neq0$. It is therefore the average of the failure probability over an independent Gaussian vector $g$ replacing $\theta$. By Fubini this is the $g$-probability, for a fixed full-rank $S$, of
$\{|\|P_Sg\|^2-n|\ge\varepsilon n\}\cup\{|\|g\|^2-n_0|\ge\varepsilon n_0\}$, and both are $\chi^2$ tails ($\|P_Sg\|^2\sim\chi^2_n$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:projectedChi} and $\|g\|^2\sim\chi^2_{n_0}$).
Outside these two events $\|(I-P_S)g\|^2=\|g\|^2-\|P_Sg\|^2$ is within $4\varepsilon\|g\|^2$ of $(1-n/n_0)\|g\|^2$, and scaling back to $\theta$ gives the claim. This route needs neither the Sherman--Morrison form of $\|(I-P_S)e_1\|^2$ nor a second inverse moment nor Haar measure.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The feature-bottleneck estimator}

Let $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ and $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ be independent Gaussian matrices, $Z=XS$, $M=S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$, $\hat\theta=My$.

\begin{theorem}[Variance of the feature-bottleneck estimator in probability]\label{thm:dd:featureVarianceProb}
  \uses{thm:dd:featureVariance, thm:dd:traceReduction, thm:dd:traceConcentration, lem:dd:invariance}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_trace_inv_gram_mul_gram_mem,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_featureTrace_deviation}
  \leanok
Fix any $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ with $S^\top S$ invertible. For every measurable $B$,
$\mathbb P_X\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}((Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S)\in B\bigr)=\mathbb P_G\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}((G^\top G)^{-1})\in B\bigr)$ for $G\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ Gaussian. Hence if $1\le n_k\le m_k$, $m_k\to\infty$ and
$n_k/m_k\to\delta<1$ then, for \emph{arbitrary} matrices $S_k$ with $S_k^\top S_k$ invertible,
$\operatorname{Tr}((Z_k^\top Z_k)^{-1}S_k^\top S_k)\to\delta/(1-\delta)$ in probability. By Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:featureVariance} the variance $V(\hat\theta)=\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}((Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S)$ therefore converges in probability to $\sigma^2\delta/(1-\delta)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Write $S=QR$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:orthFactor}) and $G=XQ$; by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceReduction} the trace equals $\operatorname{Tr}(G^\top G)^{-1}$ identically, and $G$ is Gaussian by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:invariance}(2). The limit is
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceConcentration} with $(p,m)\to(m,n)$. Neither the law of $S$ nor its independence from $X$ is used.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional concentration of the fit error of the bias]\label{thm:dd:featureBiasFixedS}
  \uses{thm:dd:biasReduction, thm:dd:fitError, lem:dd:columnSplit, lem:dd:invariance}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measurePreserving_mulVec_mul_orthonormal,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_featureBias_deviation_le}
  \leanok
Fix $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ with $S^\top S$ invertible and $\theta\in\mathbb R^{n_0}$, put $\theta_\perp=(I-P_S)\theta$ and $R_{\mathrm{bias}}=\|(MX-I)\theta\|^2$, $\nu=m-n+1$, $0<\eta<1$.
Then $R_{\mathrm{bias}}\in\|\theta_\perp\|^2\bigl[1+\tfrac{n(1-\eta)}{\nu(1+\eta)},\ 1+\tfrac{n(1+\eta)}{\nu(1-\eta)}\bigr]$ except on an $X$-event of probability at most $60/(\eta^4\nu^2)+60/(\eta^4n^2)$, whatever $S$ and $\theta$ are.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Let $S=QR$, $G=XQ$ and $u=\theta_\perp/\|\theta_\perp\|$, which is orthogonal to the columns of $Q$. The matrix $[Q\,|\,u]$ has orthonormal columns, so $X[Q\,|\,u]$ is Gaussian
(Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:invariance}(2)) and its last column $\zeta=Xu$ is independent of $G=XQ$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:columnSplit}(1); \texttt{measurePreserving\_mulVec\_mul\_orthonormal}). By
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:biasReduction}, $R_{\mathrm{bias}}=\|\theta_\perp\|^2\bigl(1+\|(G^\top G)^{-1}G^\top\zeta\|^2\bigr)$ since $X\theta_\perp=\|\theta_\perp\|\zeta$, and the fit error concentrates by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:fitError}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Bias of the feature-bottleneck estimator in probability]\label{thm:dd:featureBiasProb}
  \uses{thm:dd:featureBiasFixedS, thm:dd:projectorProbability, thm:dd:featureLimits}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_prod_sandwich_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_featureBias_joint_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.exists_tolerance_sandwich,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_feature_ratios,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_featureBias_deviation}
  \leanok
Let $1\le n_k\le\min\{m_k,n_{0,k}\}$ with $n_k\to\infty$, $n_k/m_k\to\delta<1$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>0$ and $\|\theta_k\|^2\to r$. For independent Gaussian $X_k,S_k$ and every $\varepsilon>0$,
\[
  \mathbb P\Bigl(\bigl|\,\|(M_kX_k-I)\theta_k\|^2-\tfrac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\,r\,\bigr|\ge\varepsilon\Bigr)\longrightarrow0 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Put $a_k=(1-n_k/n_{0,k})\|\theta_k\|^2\to(1-\delta/\gamma)r=:a$ and $c=\delta/(1-\delta)$, so that $\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}r=a(1+c)$ (\texttt{tendsto\_feature\_ratios}). The two-stage union bound
(\texttt{measure\_prod\_sandwich\_le}: either $\|\theta_\perp\|^2$ is far from $a_k$ on the $S$-side, or $R_{\mathrm{bias}}$ leaves its sandwich given $S$) combines Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:featureBiasFixedS}, valid for $S$-a.e.\ $S$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:fullRankAS}, with Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:projectorProbability}; this is \texttt{measure\_featureBias\_joint\_le}. A single small tolerance $t$ (\texttt{exists\_tolerance\_sandwich}:
both sides of the sandwich are continuous in $t$ and equal $a(1+c)$ at $t=0$) puts the sandwich inside $(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)$ eventually, and every failure probability tends to $0$ because $\nu_k,n_k\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The sample-bottleneck estimator: variance and bias}\label{sec:dd:sampleprob}

Throughout this subsection $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ and $V\in\mathbb R^{n\times n_0}$ are independent Gaussian matrices, $S=V^\top$ (so $S$ is a Gaussian $n_0\times n$ matrix), $Z=XS=XV^\top$, $P=P_X=\operatorname{gramProjector}X^\top$, and
$\nu=n-m+1$. The regime is $m\le\min\{n,n_0\}$, and the targets are the variance trace
$T=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((ZZ^\top)^{-1}ZS^\top SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}\bigr)$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleVariance}. By Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:sampleDecomp}(4) it splits as
\[
  T=\underbrace{\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((XX^\top)^{-1}\bigr)}_{T_1}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(B\,S^\top(I-P)S\bigr)}_{T_2},\qquad B=Z^\dagger(Z^\dagger)^\top,\quad Z^\dagger=Z^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}.
\]
The route is: $T_1$ is the inverse-Wishart trace of $X^\top$; $T_2$ is a Gaussian trace form in the residual $V(I-P)$, whose weight $B$ depends on $V$ only through $VP$, so a conditional Chebyshev bound
replaces it by its conditional mean $(n_0-m)\operatorname{Tr}B=(n_0-m)\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$; and for a \emph{fixed} design $\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$ is $\operatorname{Tr}(XX^\top)^{-1}/\nu$ up to a factor $1\pm\eta$.
The bias $\|(MX-I)\theta\|^2$ is treated in the same way at the end of this subsection.

\begin{lemma}[Two bookkeeping lemmas for tail bounds]\label{lem:dd:bookkeeping}
  \lean{MeasureTheory.measure_prod_le_of_ae_section_le, exists_pos_lt_sandwich}
  \leanok
(1) Let $\mu$ be a probability measure, $s\subseteq\alpha\times\beta$ measurable and $c\ge0$. If $\nu\{b:(a,b)\in s\}\le c$ for $\mu$-almost every $a$, then $(\mu\otimes\nu)(s)\le c$.
(2) Let $F,G:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be continuous at $0$ with $F(0)=G(0)=L$, and $\varepsilon,\delta_0>0$. Then there is a single $t\in(0,\delta_0)$ with $L-\varepsilon<F(t)$ and $G(t)<L+\varepsilon$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) Fubini: $(\mu\otimes\nu)(s)=\int\nu(s_a)\,d\mu(a)\le c$. (2) Both $\{t: L-\varepsilon<F(t)\}$ and $\{t:G(t)<L+\varepsilon\}$ are neighbourhoods of $0$, so they meet $(0,\delta_0)$ in a common point.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Sample-bottleneck trace and fitted-signal energy for a fixed design]\label{thm:dd:fixedDesignSample}
  \uses{lem:dd:orthFactor, lem:dd:invariance, thm:dd:weightedTraceConcentration, thm:dd:quadFormFixed}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.exists_frame_inv_gram_sampleDesign,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_sampleTrace_mem_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_sampleQuad_mem_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_preimage_mul_orthonormal_le}
  \leanok
Let $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ be a \emph{fixed} matrix with $XX^\top$ invertible, $V\in\mathbb R^{n\times n_0}$ Gaussian, $Z=XV^\top$, $1\le m\le n$, $\nu=n-m+1$ and $0<\eta<1$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item (Frame) There are $Q\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times m}$ with $Q^\top Q=I$ and a symmetric invertible $R$ with $R^{-1}R^{-1}=(XX^\top)^{-1}$ such that, for every $V$, $(ZZ^\top)^{-1}=R^{-1}\bigl((VQ)^\top(VQ)\bigr)^{-1}R^{-1}$.
  \item (Trace) $\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}\in\bigl[\tfrac{\operatorname{Tr}(XX^\top)^{-1}}{\nu(1+\eta)},\tfrac{\operatorname{Tr}(XX^\top)^{-1}}{\nu(1-\eta)}\bigr]$ except with probability at most $60m/(\eta^4\nu^2)$.
  \item (Energy) For every fixed $a\in\mathbb R^m$, $a^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}a\in\bigl[\tfrac{a^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}a}{\nu(1+\eta)},\tfrac{a^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}a}{\nu(1-\eta)}\bigr]$ except with probability at most $60/(\eta^4\nu^2)$.
\end{enumerate}
With $a=X\theta$ the vector $Z^\dagger a$ is the vector $b$ of interpolating coefficients of $X\theta$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:sampleDecomp}(3)), $\|b\|^2=a^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}a$, and $a^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}a=\|P_X\theta\|^2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:orthFactor} applied to $X^\top=QR$ gives $X=RQ^\top$, hence $ZZ^\top=R(VQ)^\top(VQ)R$ and $R^2=XX^\top$. (2) $G=VQ$ is Gaussian by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:invariance}, and by cyclicity $\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((XX^\top)^{-1}(G^\top G)^{-1}\bigr)$ with the positive semidefinite weight $C=(XX^\top)^{-1}$,
so Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:weightedTraceConcentration} applies with $p=n$, $q+1=m$. (3) With $\xi=R^{-1}a$ one has $a^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}a=\xi^\top(G^\top G)^{-1}\xi$ and $\|\xi\|^2=a^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}a$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:quadFormFixed} applies. The pushforward of $V$ under $V\mapsto VQ$ is the Gaussian measure (\texttt{measure\_preimage\_mul\_orthonormal\_le}).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Non-asymptotic sandwich for the sample-bottleneck variance]\label{thm:dd:sampleSandwich}
  \uses{lem:dd:sampleDecomp, thm:dd:fixedDesignSample, thm:ntkcond:conditionalChebyshev, lem:dd:bookkeeping, lem:dd:gramProjectorFacts}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_sampleTraceBad_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_sampleQuadBad_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_sampleVariance_sandwich_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.mul_transpose_mul_gramProjector_transpose,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_mul_residual_eq_sum,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.variance_sandwich_arith,
        Matrix.sum_sq_mul_transpose_le_trace_sq}
  \leanok
Let $1\le m\le\min\{n,n_0\}$, $0<\eta<1$, $0<\varepsilon_1\le c_1$, $\varepsilon_3>0$, $\lambda=(n_0-m)/\nu$, and let $T=T(X,V)$ be the variance trace above. Outside an event of probability at most
\[
  2\,\mathbb P\bigl(|\operatorname{Tr}(XX^\top)^{-1}-c_1|\ge\varepsilon_1\bigr)+\frac{120\,m}{\eta^4\nu^2}+\frac{2n_0}{\varepsilon_3^2}\Bigl(\frac{c_1+\varepsilon_1}{\nu(1-\eta)}\Bigr)^2,
\]
we have
\[
  (c_1-\varepsilon_1)\Bigl(1+\frac{\lambda}{1+\eta}\Bigr)-\varepsilon_3<T<(c_1+\varepsilon_1)\Bigl(1+\frac{\lambda}{1-\eta}\Bigr)+\varepsilon_3 .
\]
Moreover, jointly over $(X,V)$ the event ``$XX^\top$ invertible but $\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$ leaves $[\operatorname{Tr}(XX^\top)^{-1}/(\nu(1+\eta)),\operatorname{Tr}(XX^\top)^{-1}/(\nu(1-\eta))]$'' has probability at most $60m/(\eta^4\nu^2)$, and the same holds with probability $60/(\eta^4\nu^2)$ for the energy $(X\theta)^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}(X\theta)$ and any fixed $\theta\in\mathbb R^{n_0}$ (the latter is prepared for the bias and is not used in the variance bound).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Let $B_1=\{|T_1-c_1|\ge\varepsilon_1\}$ and let $B_2$ be the joint trace event; $B_2$ has probability at most $60m/(\eta^4\nu^2)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:bookkeeping}(1) applied to the sections in $V$, which are bounded by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:fixedDesignSample}(2) when $XX^\top$ is invertible
(then $\operatorname{Tr}(XX^\top)^{-1}>0$) and are empty otherwise. The energy event is handled the same way with part (3).

For the residual term write $X V^\top=X(VP)^\top$ (since $XP=X$), so $B=Z^\dagger(Z^\dagger)^\top$ is a function of $(VP,X)$ only. Theorem~\ref{thm:ntkcond:conditionalChebyshev}(3) (\texttt{NTK.conditional\_traceForm\_chebyshev}) with $A=I$, $\operatorname{tr}(I-P)=n_0-m$ and $\|I\|_F^2=n_0$ shows that
$C_3=\{|T_2-(n_0-m)\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}|\ge\varepsilon_3\}$ has probability at most $\mathbb E\min(1,2n_0\|B\|_F^2/\varepsilon_3^2)$, where $\operatorname{Tr}B=\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$ and $\|B\|_F^2\le(\operatorname{Tr}B)^2$ because $B$ is a Gram matrix
(\texttt{sum\_sq\_mul\_transpose\_le\_trace\_sq}). Off $B_1\cup B_2$ one has $\operatorname{Tr}B\le(c_1+\varepsilon_1)/(\nu(1-\eta))$, so the integrand is at most the constant $2n_0\bigl((c_1+\varepsilon_1)/(\nu(1-\eta))\bigr)^2/\varepsilon_3^2$ there and at most $1$ on $B_1\cup B_2$. This gives
$\mathbb P(C_3)\le\mathbb P(B_1)+\mathbb P(B_2)+{}$the constant, and a union bound over $B_1,B_2,C_3$ gives the stated total. Off the three events, $T_1\in(c_1-\varepsilon_1,c_1+\varepsilon_1)$, $\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}\in[T_1/(\nu(1+\eta)),T_1/(\nu(1-\eta))]$ and $T_2$ is within $\varepsilon_3$ of $(n_0-m)\operatorname{Tr}(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$,
and the arithmetic of Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:sampleDecomp}(4) with $T=T_1+T_2$ yields the sandwich (\texttt{variance\_sandwich\_arith}).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Ratio limits and the design trace beyond the threshold]\label{thm:dd:sampleRatios}
  \uses{thm:dd:traceConcentration}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_sample_ratios,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_design_trace_inv_deviation,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_preimage_transpose_le}
  \leanok
(1) If $1\le m_k\le n_k$, $m_k\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma$ and $n_k/m_k\to\delta>1$, then for $\nu_k=n_k-m_k+1$: $\nu_k\to\infty$, $\nu_k/m_k\to\delta-1$, $m_k/\nu_k^2\to0$, $n_{0,k}/\nu_k^2\to0$ and $(n_{0,k}-m_k)/\nu_k\to(\gamma-1)/(\delta-1)$.
(2) If $m_k\le n_{0,k}$, $m_k\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>1$ and $X_k$ is an $m_k\times n_{0,k}$ Gaussian matrix, then for every $\varepsilon>0$, $\mathbb P\bigl(|\operatorname{Tr}(X_kX_k^\top)^{-1}-(\gamma-1)^{-1}|\ge\varepsilon\bigr)\to0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) is arithmetic with the limits of $\nu_k/m_k=n_k/m_k-1+1/m_k$. (2) Apply the in-probability part of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceConcentration} to $W=X_k^\top\in\mathbb R^{n_{0,k}\times m_k}$ (transposing preserves the Gaussian law, \texttt{measure\_preimage\_transpose\_le}), with $\rho=\gamma^{-1}<1$ and
$\rho/(1-\rho)=(\gamma-1)^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The sample-bottleneck variance converges in probability]\label{thm:dd:sampleVarianceProb}
  \uses{thm:dd:sampleSandwich, thm:dd:sampleRatios, lem:dd:bookkeeping, thm:dd:sampleVariance}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_sampleVariance_deviation_transposed,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_sampleVariance_deviation}
  \leanok
Let $0<m_k\le\min\{n_k,n_{0,k}\}$ with $m_k\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>1$ and $n_k/m_k\to\delta>1$; let $X_k\in\mathbb R^{m_k\times n_{0,k}}$ and $V_k\in\mathbb R^{n_k\times n_{0,k}}$ be independent Gaussian matrices, $S_k=V_k^\top$, $Z_k=X_kS_k$, and let $T_k$ be the variance trace of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleVariance}. Then for every $\varepsilon>0$
\[
  \mathbb P\Bigl(\bigl|T_k-\bigl((\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}\bigr)\bigr|\ge\varepsilon\Bigr)\longrightarrow0 .
\]
The same holds with the Gaussian matrix $S_k\in\mathbb R^{n_{0,k}\times n_k}$ in place of $V_k=S_k^\top$ (\texttt{tendsto\_measure\_sampleVariance\_deviation}; the two laws agree by transposition invariance, \texttt{measure\_prod\_preimage\_transpose\_le}). Consequently the variance $\sigma^2T_k$ of $\hat\theta_k=S_kZ_k^\dagger y$ converges in probability to $\sigma^2\bigl((\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}\bigr)$, the limit of the explicit expression in Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleLimits}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Put $c_1=(\gamma-1)^{-1}$ and $\lambda=(\gamma-1)/(\delta-1)$, so that $c_1(1+\lambda)=(\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}$. Define $F(t)=(c_1-t)\bigl(1+\lambda/(1+t)\bigr)-t$ and $G(t)=(c_1+t)\bigl(1+\lambda/(1-t)\bigr)+t$; both are continuous at $0$ with value $c_1(1+\lambda)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:bookkeeping}(2) gives one $t\in(0,\min(1,c_1))$
with $F(t)>L-\varepsilon$ and $G(t)<L+\varepsilon$, where $L=c_1(1+\lambda)$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleSandwich} with $\eta=\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_3=t$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleRatios}(1), $(n_{0,k}-m_k)/\nu_k\to\lambda$, so the endpoints of the sandwich converge to $F(t)$ and $G(t)$ and
eventually lie in $(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)$. It remains to see that the failure probability tends to $0$: the first term does by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleRatios}(2), the second is $120m_k/(t^4\nu_k^2)\to0$ and the third is $2n_{0,k}(\cdots)^2/(t^2\nu_k^2)\to0$, both by $m_k/\nu_k^2\to0$ and $n_{0,k}/\nu_k^2\to0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Non-asymptotic sandwich for the sample-bottleneck bias]\label{thm:dd:sampleBiasSandwich}
  \uses{lem:dd:sampleDecomp, thm:dd:fixedDesignSample, thm:ntkcond:conditionalChebyshev, lem:dd:bookkeeping, thm:dd:projectorProbability}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_sampleBias_sandwich_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.norm_sq_sub_expand,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.design_quadForm_inv_eq,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.bias_sandwich_arith}
  \leanok
Let $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ and $V\in\mathbb R^{n\times n_0}$ be independent Gaussian matrices, $S=V^\top$, $Z=XS$, $1\le m\le\min\{n,n_0\}$, $\nu=n-m+1$, $r=\|\theta\|^2$, and let $R_b=\|(MX-I)\theta\|^2$ be the bias of $\hat\theta=SZ^\dagger y$, $M=SZ^\dagger$. With $d=\|(I-P_X)\theta\|^2$, $d_0=(1-m/n_0)r$, $s_0=(m/n_0)r$, $\lambda=(n_0-m)/\nu$, outside an event of probability at most
\[
  3\,\mathbb P\bigl(XX^\top\text{ singular}\bigr)+\mathbb P\bigl(|d-d_0|\ge\varepsilon_5\bigr)+\frac{180\,m}{\eta^4\nu^2}+\frac{180}{\eta^4\nu^2}+\frac{2n_0}{\varepsilon_6^2}\Bigl(\frac{r}{\nu(1-\eta)}\Bigr)^2+\frac{r^2}{\nu(1-\eta)\varepsilon_7^2},
\]
we have
\[
  d_0-\varepsilon_5+\lambda\frac{s_0-\varepsilon_5}{1+\eta}-(\varepsilon_6+2\varepsilon_7)<R_b<d_0+\varepsilon_5+\lambda\frac{s_0+\varepsilon_5}{1-\eta}+(\varepsilon_6+2\varepsilon_7).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
By Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:sampleDecomp}(3), $(MX-I)\theta=(I-P)Sb-(I-P)\theta$ with $b=Z^\dagger X\theta$. The squared norm expands as $R_b=d+b^\top(VP^\perp)(VP^\perp)^\top b-2b^\top(VP^\perp)\theta$ (\texttt{norm\_sq\_sub\_expand}). The weight $b$ depends on $V$ only through $VP$ and $X$. Conditional Chebyshev bounds control the quadratic and linear forms in $VP^\perp$ (\texttt{NTK.conditional\_quadForm\_chebyshev}, \texttt{NTK.conditional\_linearForm\_chebyshev}), $\|b\|^2=(X\theta)^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}(X\theta)$ is bounded by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleSandwich}, and $(X\theta)^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}(X\theta)=r-d$ (\texttt{design\_quadForm\_inv\_eq}). The arithmetic of \texttt{bias\_sandwich\_arith} gives the sandwich.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The sample-bottleneck bias converges in probability]\label{thm:dd:sampleBiasProb}
  \uses{thm:dd:sampleBiasSandwich, thm:dd:sampleRatios, lem:dd:bookkeeping, thm:dd:projectorProbability, thm:dd:sampleLimits}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_sampleBias_deviation_transposed,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_sampleBias_deviation}
  \leanok
Let $0<m_k\le\min\{n_k,n_{0,k}\}$ with $m_k\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>1$ and $n_k/m_k\to\delta>1$; let $X_k\in\mathbb R^{m_k\times n_{0,k}}$ and $V_k\in\mathbb R^{n_k\times n_{0,k}}$ be independent Gaussian matrices, $S_k=V_k^\top$, $Z_k=X_kS_k$, and let $\|\theta_k\|^2\to r$. Then for every $\varepsilon>0$
\[
  \mathbb P\Bigl(\bigl|\|(M_kX_k-I)\theta_k\|^2-(1-\gamma^{-1})\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}r\bigr|\ge\varepsilon\Bigr)\longrightarrow0 .
\]
The same holds with the Gaussian matrix $S_k\in\mathbb R^{n_{0,k}\times n_k}$ in place of $V_k=S_k^\top$ (\texttt{tendsto\_measure\_sampleBias\_deviation}; the two laws agree by transposition invariance, \texttt{measure\_prod\_preimage\_transpose\_le}). Consequently the bias of $\hat\theta_k=S_kZ_k^\dagger y$ converges in probability to $(1-\gamma^{-1})\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}\|\theta_\star\|^2$, the limit of the explicit expression in Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleLimits}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Put $\lambda=(\gamma-1)/(\delta-1)$ and $L=(1-\gamma^{-1})r+\lambda(\gamma^{-1}r)=(1-\gamma^{-1})\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}r$. Define $F(t)=(1-\gamma^{-1})r-t+\lambda(\gamma^{-1}r-t)/(1+t)-3t$ and $G(t)=(1-\gamma^{-1})r+t+\lambda(\gamma^{-1}r+t)/(1-t)+3t$; both are continuous at $0$ with value $L$. Choose $t\in(0,1)$ via Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:bookkeeping}(2) with $F(t)>L-\varepsilon$ and $G(t)<L+\varepsilon$, and instantiate Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleBiasSandwich} with $\eta=\varepsilon_5=\varepsilon_6=\varepsilon_7=t$. The endpoints converge to $F(t)$ and $G(t)$ and eventually enter $(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)$. The failure probabilities tend to $0$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleRatios}, Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:projectorProbability} (omitted signal of $P_X$ via $X_k^\top$), $m_k/\nu_k^2\to0$, and $n_{0,k}/\nu_k^2\to0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is and is not formalized in the probabilistic layer]\label{rmk:dd:remaining}
  \uses{thm:dd:featureVarianceProb, thm:dd:featureBiasProb, thm:dd:ambientBV, thm:dd:sampleVarianceProb, thm:dd:sampleBiasProb, thm:dd:fixedDesignSample, thm:dd:regimeRisks, thm:dd:globalDoubleDescent}
\emph{Feature bottleneck.} Variance and bias converge in probability to $\sigma^2\delta/(1-\delta)$ and $\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho_\star$ (Theorems~\ref{thm:dd:featureVarianceProb} and~\ref{thm:dd:featureBiasProb}), which are the limits
the deterministic layer takes for the explicit expressions (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:featureLimits}).

\emph{Ambient bottleneck.} The estimator is OLS, unbiased, with variance $\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}(X^\top X)^{-1}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:ambientBV}); the convergence to $\sigma^2\gamma/(1-\gamma)$ is
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceConcentration} with $(p,m)\to(m,n_0)$. It has no separate statement about the variance alone; it enters only through the risk statement Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:regimeRisks}(3).

\emph{Sample bottleneck.} Both the \textbf{variance} and the \textbf{bias} converge in probability to $\sigma^2\bigl((\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}\bigr)$ and $(1-\gamma^{-1})\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}\rho_\star$ (Theorems~\ref{thm:dd:sampleVarianceProb} and~\ref{thm:dd:sampleBiasProb}), which are the limits of the explicit expressions of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:sampleLimits}. The argument does not treat $ZZ^\top$ as the Gram matrix of one Gaussian matrix: the variance splits into the inverse-Wishart trace of $X^\top$ and a residual trace form (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:sampleDecomp}(4)), and the bias into the omitted signal of $P_X$ and residual quadratic and linear forms;
the residual forms are controlled by conditional Chebyshev bounds, without choosing coordinates for $\ker X$, and the fixed-design traces are reduced to a weighted inverse Gram trace (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:fixedDesignSample}).

\emph{Global statement.} The three regimes are combined in Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:globalDoubleDescent}, as convergence in probability of the conditional risk to $\mathsf R(\gamma,\delta)$ on the non-singular domain. \textbf{Not formalized:} the singular set $\gamma=1$ or $\delta=1$ (there $\mathsf R$ is infinite, and the Lean curve has the junk value $x/0=0$); the diagonal $\gamma=\delta<1$ requires the extra hypothesis that eventually $n_k\le n_{0,k}$ or eventually $n_{0,k}<n_k$ (otherwise one splits the index set);
convergence of the risk in expectation over $(X,S)$ rather than in probability; and any non-Gaussian design or feature distribution.

\emph{Unused deterministic lemma.} The decomposition $\operatorname{Tr}W^{-2}=\sum_jW^{-1}_{jj}{}^2(1+\|b_j\|^2)$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceSecond} was prepared for a second-moment route to the quadratic form $s^\top W^{-1}s$; the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:fitError} gives
the concentration directly from the exact law of the Rayleigh quotient, so no second inverse moment is needed and no later file uses Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceSecond}.
\end{remark}

\section{The global double-descent theorem}\label{sec:dd:global}

This section assembles the three regimes. Its Lean file is \texttt{DoubleDescent/DoubleDescentLimits.lean}, with the sum-of-deviations lemma in \texttt{ForMathlib/MeasureTheory/Measure/Deviation.lean}. Until now the estimator was
always an abstract \texttt{β\_hat} satisfying a hypothesis; here the matrix $M$ and the risk become Lean definitions.

\begin{definition}[Minimum-norm random-feature operator and conditional risk]\label{def:dd:minNormOperator}
  \uses{def:dd:randomFeatures, def:dd:risk}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.minNormOperator,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.conditionalRisk}
  \leanok
For $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n_0}$ and $S\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times n}$ put $Z=XS$ and define $M=M(X,S)\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times m}$ by the \emph{smallest of the three dimensions}:
\[
  M=\begin{cases}
  S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top & n\le\min\{m,n_0\}\quad\text{(feature bottleneck)},\\[0.5ex]
  S\bigl(S^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}\bigr)\bigl((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\bigr) & \text{otherwise, if } n_0\le\min\{m,n\}\quad\text{(ambient bottleneck)},\\[0.5ex]
  SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1} & \text{otherwise}\quad\text{(sample bottleneck)},
  \end{cases}
\]
with Mathlib's inverse (so a singular matrix is replaced by $0$). This is the matrix $M$ of the last column of the table in Definition~\ref{def:dd:randomFeatures}, selected by the dimensions instead of by the reader. The \emph{conditional risk} is
\[
  \mathsf{cr}(\sigma^2,\theta,X,S)=\|(MX-I)\theta\|^2+\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M),
\]
the bias plus the variance of the estimator $My$ with isotropic noise.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[The regime formulas for the conditional risk]\label{lem:dd:globalBranches}
  \uses{def:dd:minNormOperator, thm:dd:linearBV, thm:dd:featureVariance, thm:dd:ambientBV, thm:dd:sampleVariance, lem:dd:leftInverse, lem:dd:rightInverse}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.minNormOperator_of_feature,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.minNormOperator_of_ambient,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.minNormOperator_of_sample,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.conditionalRisk_eq_integral,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.trace_featureOperator_eq,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.conditionalRisk_of_feature,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.conditionalRisk_of_sample,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.conditionalRisk_of_ambient}
  \leanok
\begin{enumerate}
  \item (Bridge) If $\varepsilon$ has mean $0$ and covariance $\sigma^2I$, and $\hat\theta(\varepsilon)=M(X\theta+\varepsilon)$ with $M=M(X,S)$, then $\mathbb E\|\hat\theta-\theta\|^2=\mathsf{cr}(\sigma^2,\theta,X,S)$.
  \item (Feature) If $n\le\min\{m,n_0\}$, then $\mathsf{cr}=\|(MX-I)\theta\|^2+\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((Z^\top Z)^{-1}S^\top S\bigr)$ with $M=S(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$; the trace identity holds without any invertibility hypothesis, since both sides vanish for singular $Z^\top Z$.
  \item (Sample) If $n>\min\{m,n_0\}$ and $n_0>\min\{m,n\}$, then $\mathsf{cr}=\|(MX-I)\theta\|^2+\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((ZZ^\top)^{-1}ZS^\top SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}\bigr)$ with $M=SZ^\top(ZZ^\top)^{-1}$.
  \item (Ambient) If $n\not\le\min\{m,n_0\}$, $n_0\le\min\{m,n\}$ and both $X^\top X$ and $SS^\top$ are invertible, then $\mathsf{cr}=\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}(X^\top X)^{-1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) is Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:linearBV} with the vector norm expanded as a dot product. (2) and (3) unfold the case distinction and use the trace reductions of Lemmas~\ref{lem:dd:featureTrace} and~\ref{lem:dd:sampleTrace}. (4) $S\bigl(S^\top(SS^\top)^{-1}\bigr)=I$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:rightInverse}),
so $M=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$, then $MX=I$ and $\operatorname{Tr}(M^\top M)=\operatorname{Tr}(X^\top X)^{-1}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:leftInverse}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Deviation of a sum]\label{lem:dd:deviationSum}
  \lean{MeasureTheory.measure_add_mul_deviation_le,
        MeasureTheory.tendsto_measure_add_mul_deviation}
  \leanok
Let $\mu$ be a measure and $R=A+\sigma B$ with real-valued $A,B$. Then for $\varepsilon>0$,
\[
  \mu\{|R-(a_0+\sigma b_0)|\ge\varepsilon\}\le\mu\{|A-a_0|\ge\tfrac\varepsilon2\}+\mu\Bigl\{|B-b_0|\ge\tfrac\varepsilon{2(|\sigma|+1)}\Bigr\}.
\]
Hence if $A_k\to a_0$ and $B_k\to b_0$ in probability and $R_k=A_k+\sigma B_k$, then $R_k\to a_0+\sigma b_0$ in probability.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
If $|A-a_0|<\varepsilon/2$ and $|B-b_0|<\varepsilon/(2(|\sigma|+1))$, then $|R-(a_0+\sigma b_0)|\le|A-a_0|+|\sigma||B-b_0|<\varepsilon$. A union bound gives the first claim, and the second follows by squeezing between $0$ and the sum of the two vanishing probabilities.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Feature bottleneck, jointly over the data and the features]\label{thm:dd:featureJoint}
  \uses{thm:dd:featureVarianceProb, thm:dd:featureBiasProb, thm:dd:traceConcentration, thm:dd:projectorProbability}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.measure_featureVariance_joint_le,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_featureVariance_deviation,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_featureBias_deviation,
        MeasureTheory.measure_prod_swap_le}
  \leanok
Let $X_k\in\mathbb R^{m_k\times n_{0,k}}$ and $S_k\in\mathbb R^{n_{0,k}\times n_k}$ be independent Gaussian matrices, $1\le n_k\le\min\{m_k,n_{0,k}\}$, $m_k\to\infty$ and $n_k/m_k\to\delta<1$. Then, with $Z_k=X_kS_k$, $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((Z_k^\top Z_k)^{-1}S_k^\top S_k\bigr)\to\delta/(1-\delta)$ in probability, jointly in $(X_k,S_k)$.
If moreover $n_k\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>0$ and $\|\theta_k\|^2\to\rho$, then $\|(M_kX_k-I)\theta_k\|^2\to\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho$ in probability, with $M_k=S_k(Z_k^\top Z_k)^{-1}Z_k^\top$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
For a fixed realization of $S$ (almost surely of full column rank), the variance trace has the law of $\operatorname{Tr}(G^\top G)^{-1}$ for an $m\times n$ Gaussian matrix $G$; this is the $S$-section bound \texttt{measure\_featureVariance\_joint\_le}, which together with the product-measure lemma \texttt{measure\_prod\_le\_of\_ae\_section\_le}
gives the joint bound and reduces the limit to Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceConcentration}. The bias statement is Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:featureBiasProb}, whose proof conditions on $S$ and therefore exchanges the two factors of the product measure once (\texttt{measure\_prod\_swap\_le}), so that all regimes are stated in the order $(X,S)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The risk converges in probability in each regime]\label{thm:dd:regimeRisks}
  \uses{lem:dd:globalBranches, lem:dd:deviationSum, thm:dd:featureJoint, thm:dd:sampleVarianceProb, thm:dd:sampleBiasProb, thm:dd:traceConcentration}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_featureRisk_deviation,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_sampleRisk_deviation,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_measure_ambientRisk_deviation}
  \leanok
Let $X_k\in\mathbb R^{m_k\times n_{0,k}}$, $S_k\in\mathbb R^{n_{0,k}\times n_k}$ be independent Gaussian matrices, $\|\theta_k\|^2\to\rho$, and write $\mathsf{cr}_k=\mathsf{cr}(\sigma^2,\theta_k,X_k,S_k)$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ the probability that $\mathsf{cr}_k$ deviates by $\varepsilon$ or more from the stated limit tends to $0$ in each of the following cases, where the inequalities between $m_k,n_k,n_{0,k}$ are only required for all large $k$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item (Feature) $1\le n_k\le\min\{m_k,n_{0,k}\}$, $n_k\to\infty$, $n_k/m_k\to\delta<1$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>0$: limit $\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho+\sigma^2\frac\delta{1-\delta}$.
  \item (Sample) $m_k<\min\{n_{0,k},n_k\}$, $m_k\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>1$, $n_k/m_k\to\delta>1$: limit $(1-\gamma^{-1})\frac\delta{\delta-1}\rho+\sigma^2\bigl((\gamma-1)^{-1}+(\delta-1)^{-1}\bigr)$.
  \item (Ambient) $0<n_{0,k}\le m_k$, $n_{0,k}<n_k$, $n_{0,k}\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma\in(0,1)$ (any $\theta_k$): limit $\sigma^2\gamma/(1-\gamma)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
(1) and (2): in the respective regime the minimum-norm operator is the feature, respectively sample, formula (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:globalBranches}), so $\mathsf{cr}_k=A_k+\sigma^2B_k$ with $A_k$ the bias and $B_k$ the variance trace. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:deviationSum} with the limits of
Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:featureJoint}, respectively Theorems~\ref{thm:dd:sampleBiasProb} and~\ref{thm:dd:sampleVarianceProb}.
Throughout, shift the sequences by the index $K$ from which the inequalities hold; convergence is unaffected by dropping finitely many terms.
(3) The matrices $S_k^\top$ and $X_k$ have full rank almost surely (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:fullRankAS}; \texttt{ae\_isUnit\_det\_mul\_transpose\_gaussianMatrix} for $S_kS_k^\top$, using $n_{0,k}<n_k$). On this event, if $\operatorname{Tr}(X_k^\top X_k)^{-1}$ is within $\eta=\min\{\varepsilon/(|\sigma^2|+1),\gamma/(1-\gamma)\}$ of
$\gamma/(1-\gamma)$ then it is positive, so $X_k^\top X_k$ is invertible and Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:globalBranches}(4) gives $\mathsf{cr}_k=\sigma^2\operatorname{Tr}(X_k^\top X_k)^{-1}$, within $\varepsilon$ of $\sigma^2\gamma/(1-\gamma)$. The bad event is therefore contained in $\{|\operatorname{Tr}(X_k^\top X_k)^{-1}-\gamma/(1-\gamma)|\ge\eta\}$, whose probability tends to $0$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:traceConcentration} (this is the ambient limit that earlier had no Lean statement).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Ratio bookkeeping for the global theorem]\label{lem:dd:globalRatios}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_ratio_of_ratios,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.tendsto_atTop_of_ratio,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.eventually_lt_of_ratio,
        LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.eventually_gt_of_ratio}
  \leanok
(1) If $a_k/c_k\to\alpha$ and $b_k/c_k\to\beta\ne0$ then $a_k/b_k\to\alpha/\beta$. (2) If $a_k/c_k\to\alpha>0$ and $c_k\to\infty$ then $a_k\to\infty$. (3) If $a_k/b_k\to\alpha<1$ then $a_k<b_k$ eventually; if $\alpha>1$ then $b_k<a_k$ eventually.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
These are elementary limit arguments (multiply by $c_k$, respectively compare $a_k/b_k$ with $1$).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Global double descent of the risk, in probability]\label{thm:dd:globalDoubleDescent}
  \uses{def:dd:curve, lem:dd:branches, thm:dd:regimeRisks, lem:dd:globalRatios}
  \lean{LinearRegression.DoubleDescent.double_descent_total_risk_convergence}
  \leanok
Let $m_k\to\infty$, $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>0$, $n_k/m_k\to\delta>0$, $\|\theta_k\|^2\to\rho$, and suppose $(\gamma,\delta)$ lies in the non-singular domain
\[
  \delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}\quad\text{or}\quad(\gamma<1\ \text{and}\ \gamma\le\delta)\quad\text{or}\quad(\gamma>1\ \text{and}\ \delta>1).
\]
On the diagonal $\gamma=\delta<1$ assume in addition that eventually $n_k\le n_{0,k}$ or eventually $n_{0,k}<n_k$. Let $X_k\in\mathbb R^{m_k\times n_{0,k}}$ and $S_k\in\mathbb R^{n_{0,k}\times n_k}$ be independent Gaussian matrices. Then for every $\varepsilon>0$
\[
  \mathbb P\Bigl(\bigl|\mathsf{cr}(\sigma^2,\theta_k,X_k,S_k)-\mathsf R(\gamma,\delta)\bigr|\ge\varepsilon\Bigr)\longrightarrow0,
\]
where $\mathsf R=\mathsf B+\mathsf V$ is the limiting risk of Definition~\ref{def:dd:curve}. In particular, on the non-singular domain the random risk follows the deterministic curve $\mathsf R$ whose shape is described in Section~\ref{sec:dd:curve}: the divergence as $\delta\to1$ and the second descent for $\gamma>1$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:doubleDescent}), and the flat segment for $\gamma<1$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:undercomplete}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
Since $n_{0,k}/m_k\to\gamma>0$ and $n_k/m_k\to\delta>0$, both $n_{0,k}$ and $n_k$ tend to infinity (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:globalRatios}(2)). In the feature domain $\delta<\min\{1,\gamma\}$, the ratios give $n_k\le m_k$ and $n_k\le n_{0,k}$ eventually (Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:globalRatios}(1) and~(3)), so Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:regimeRisks}(1) applies, and its limit is $\mathsf R(\gamma,\delta)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:dd:branches}.
In the ambient domain $\gamma<1$, $\gamma\le\delta$: eventually $n_{0,k}\le m_k$; if $\gamma<\delta$ then eventually $n_{0,k}<n_k$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:regimeRisks}(3) applies; if $\gamma=\delta$ the extra hypothesis selects either the ambient argument, or the feature argument, which has the same limit because at $\gamma=\delta$ the feature bias $\frac{\gamma-\delta}{\gamma(1-\delta)}\rho$ vanishes and the feature variance $\sigma^2\frac\delta{1-\delta}$ equals the ambient one. In the sample domain $\gamma,\delta>1$, eventually $m_k<n_{0,k}$ and $m_k<n_k$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:dd:regimeRisks}(2) applies.
\end{proof}

The excluded set is $\{\gamma\ge1,\ \delta\ge1,\ \gamma=1\vee\delta=1\}$, where the limiting risk is infinite (the interpolation peak) and the junk value $x/0=0$ of the curve would make a statement false. At the boundary $\gamma=\delta<1$ the ordering hypothesis is genuinely used: the operator $M$ switches branches according to whether $n_k\le n_{0,k}$.
